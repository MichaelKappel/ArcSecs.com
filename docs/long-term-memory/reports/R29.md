## **3\. Literature Review Across Four Observation Classes**

A viable physical-process law must satisfy empirical constraints established across four independent observational domains spanning laboratory scales of less than one millimeter to orbital baselines of tens of thousands of kilometers.

### **3.1 Gravitational Frequency Shifts in Optical, Nuclear, and Mössbauer Clocks**

Gravitational frequency offsets were first measured on Earth using the recoil-free 14.4-kiloelectronvolt nuclear gamma transition of iron-57 across a 22.5-meter vertical path in Harvard's Jefferson Tower28. Pound and Rebka established the gravitational frequency shift to roughly ten percent precision in 196029, and Pound and Snider refined the measurement to approximately one percent in 1965, matching the expected fractional shift of 2.455 parts in 10¹⁵ one-way (4.910 parts in 10¹⁵ two-way)1. Critically, Mössbauer experiments probe nuclear energy-level differences mediated by gamma-ray propagation between two elevations33.  
Over the past fifteen years, optical atomic clocks have improved resolution by more than five orders of magnitude. Comparing two single trapped aluminum-27 ion clocks operating near 1.121 PHz (quality factor 4.2 × 10¹⁴, systematic uncertainty 8.6 × 10⁻¹⁸) connected by a 75-meter optical fiber, Chou et al. resolved the fractional frequency offset across a 0.33-meter height change—corresponding to 3.597 parts in 10¹⁷—as well as velocity-induced shifts at root-mean-square speeds below ten meters per second1. Using transportable strontium-87 optical lattice clocks operating near 429.228 THz, Grotti et al. demonstrated field geopotential metrology36, and Takamoto et al. compared two clocks separated vertically by 452.631 ± 0.013 m (laser ranging; 452.650 ± 0.039 m by spirit levelling) at Tokyo Skytree6. Controlling blackbody radiation shifts with a 245-kelvin four-stage Peltier thermal shield and cancelling multipolar and hyperpolarizability lattice light shifts to one part in 10¹⁸ at a magic trap depth of 81 recoil energies (lattice laser tuned to 368,554,470.4 ± 0.2 MHz), Takamoto et al. measured a beat shift of approximately 21.18 Hz, bounding the local position invariance (LPI) deviation parameter alpha to (1.4 ± 9.1) × 10⁻⁵1.  
At sub-centimeter scales, two landmark 2022 experiments resolved gravitational frequency gradients inside single ensembles of strontium-87 atoms, rejecting common-mode laser noise5. Zheng et al. multiplexed spatially separated strontium ensembles to measure a gradient consistent with −10.9 × 10⁻¹⁹ per cm7. Bothwell et al. achieved a fractional frequency measurement uncertainty of 7.6 × 10⁻²¹ across a one-millimeter cloud; after subtracting differential density, Stark, blackbody, and lattice-tilt systematics from the raw −1.00(12) × 10⁻¹⁹ per mm slope, they measured a linear frequency gradient of −9.8(2.3) × 10⁻²⁰ per mm, consistent with the Boulder local reference gradient of −1.090 × 10⁻¹⁹ per mm1. Coherent delocalization techniques in ytterbium-171 optical lattice clocks subsequently reduced cold-collision shifts by a factor of 6.5(8)40, and spin-squeezing modeling projects resolving gravitational shifts down to 0.48 mm effective separation41.  
Crucially for cross-sector physics, optical spectroscopy achieved the first laser excitation of the unique 8.36 eV low-energy nuclear isomeric transition in thorium-229 doped into solid-state crystals in 2024\. Tiedau et al. measured the resonance at 2,020.409(7) THz (148.3821(5) nm, 8.35574(3) eV) in calcium fluoride with a crystal fluorescence lifetime of 630(15) s showing no detectable variation between 108 K and 168 K15, and Elwell et al. measured 8.355733 ± 0.000002 (statistical) ± 0.000010 (systematic) eV in a fluoride host crystal43. Months later, Zhang et al. linked a vacuum-ultraviolet frequency comb to an optical strontium-87 lattice clock, determining the thorium-229 absolute frequency 2,020,407,384,335(2) kHz and the nuclear-to-atomic frequency ratio 4.707072615078(5) in calcium fluoride at 150(1) K2, followed by a February 2026 study reporting solid-state frequency reproducibility14.

### **3.2 Transportable Clocks, GNSS Satellites, and Kinematic Corrections**

Spaceborne and airborne clocks probe combined gravitational potential differences and macroscopic translational speeds over long baselines. In 1971, Hafele and Keating flew four cesium-beam atomic clocks around the world in commercial aircraft and compared accumulated cycle counts against reference clocks at the U.S. Naval Observatory, recording a net loss of 59 ± 10 ns on the eastward circumnavigation and a net gain of \+273 ± 7 ns on the westward circumnavigation, consistent with the \+275 ± 21 ns westbound reference prediction18. A June 2010 round-the-world repetition by the National Physical Laboratory recorded \+230 ± 20 ns against a reference prediction of \+246 ± 3 ns18.  
In 1976, the Gravity Probe A rocket experiment launched a hydrogen maser into an approximately two-hour parabolic trajectory, constraining LPI violations to an uncertainty of 1.4 × 10⁻⁴ (about 0.01 percent accuracy)16. That benchmark stood for over four decades until the launch of two European Galileo navigation satellites (GSAT0201 and GSAT0202) into eccentric orbits (46,584 s period) produced a periodic gravitational modulation of approximately 400 ns peak-to-peak on top of a nominal 40 µs/day linear drift (with an intentional −6 µs/day frequency offset)8. Analyzing 1,008 days of satellite clock data calibrated by dedicated laser ranging and thermal and magnetic modeling, Delva et al. bounded the LPI violation parameter at (0.19 ± 2.48) × 10⁻⁵8, confirmed independently by Herrmann et al. at (4.5 ± 3.1) × 10⁻⁵9.  
Operational Global Positioning System (GPS) navigation provides continuous engineering verification of both gravitational and motional rate offsets. As documented by Ashby, a satellite clock orbiting at radius 26,561.75 km and speed 3,873.83 m/s exhibits a net fractional frequency excess of roughly \+4.465 × 10⁻¹⁰ (approximately \+39,000 ns/day; an idealized spherical-Earth calculation yields \+4.4687 × 10⁻¹⁰ or 38,609.8 ns/day, comprising \+45,822.9 ns/day from the weaker orbital gravitational potential and −7,213.1 ns/day from orbital velocity relative to a reference clock on Earth's rotating geoid)1. System engineers permanently tune satellite synthesizers downward on the ground from 10.23 MHz to 10.229999995453 MHz so that orbital rates match terrestrial receivers, while receivers apply Sagnac path-propagation corrections reaching hundreds of nanoseconds49. Kentosh and Mohageg further analyzed GPS orbital eccentricity variations to bound spatial variability of clock frequencies and Planck's constant51.

### **3.3 Fast Muon Lifetimes and Accelerator Decay Experiments**

Unstable elementary particles test whether non-electromagnetic weak-interaction decay rates obey the same kinematic scaling as atomic oscillators. Rossi and Hall (1941) first showed that atmospheric cosmic-ray muons (then termed mesotrons) detected between Echo Lake (3,240 m) and Denver (1,616 m) suffered anomalous attenuation in air compared with dense iron absorbers; separating soft (196–311 g/cm² lead range) from hard (greater than 311 g/cm²) momentum bands revealed that lower-momentum muons decayed roughly three times more rapidly over identical path lengths, yielding a rest lifetime estimate of (2.4 ± 0.3) µs52. Frisch and Smith (1963) refined this atmospheric survival comparison55.  
The most exacting particle-decay benchmark remains the CERN Muon Storage Ring experiment of Bailey et al. (1977, 1979\)11. Injecting spin-polarized positive and negative muons at momentum 3.098 GeV/c (Lorentz factor 29.327(4), speed approximately 0.9994 of light speed) into a 14-meter-diameter uniform magnetic storage ring, the collaboration measured positron and electron decay curves yielding 64.419(58) µs for positive muons and 64.368(29) µs for negative muons, for a combined mean orbital lifetime of 64.378(26) µs1. Compared against the 1977 rest lifetime of 2.1948(10) µs, the predicted value of 64.367 µs agrees within 0.011 µs; against the modern Particle Data Group rest lifetime of 2.1969811 µs, the predicted value of 64.431 µs sits 0.053 µs (0.82 parts per thousand) from the combined measurement, confirming weak-decay scaling to 0.9 ± 0.4 parts per thousand even under laboratory centripetal accelerations of roughly 1.3 × 10¹⁶ m/s² along the 7-meter storage-ring radius1. The modern Fermilab Muon g-2 Experiment (E989) routinely operates at the identical magic Lorentz factor (\~29.3, 3.1 GeV, 1.45 T ring) to measure the muon magnetic anomaly to sub-part-per-million precision, reaching 0.001165920705 ± 0.000000000114 (statistical) ± 0.000000000091 (systematic) in June 2025 while relying continuously on the \~64.4 µs decay distribution of stored muons12.

### **3.4 Transverse Doppler Spectroscopy and Multi-Species Equivalence Tests**

Ives–Stilwell-type experiments eliminate first-order longitudinal Doppler shifts by simultaneously probing forward and backward optical transitions in high-speed ion beams. At the GSI Experimental Storage Ring, Botermann et al. (2014) locked lasers to collinear and anti-collinear transitions of stored lithium-7 ions circulating at 0.338 times the speed of light (Lorentz factor 1.062534), verifying the standard kinematic frequency-scaling relation to within ±2.3 × 10⁻⁹1.  
Long-baseline multi-species comparisons test whether different physical interactions—electronic Coulomb, hyperfine magnetic, and nuclear strong forces—scale identically under changing gravitational potentials. Comparing four NIST hydrogen masers against cesium fountains across seven years as Earth's eccentric orbit modulated the Sun's gravitational potential by 1.66 × 10⁻¹⁰, Ashby et al. (2018) found no correlation with solar potential variations61. Over fourteen years of rubidium-87 and cesium-133 fountain comparisons across a solar potential modulation of 1.65 × 10⁻¹⁰, the LNE-SYRTE collaboration constrained the differential gravitational coupling parameter between rubidium and cesium to (0.11 ± 1.04) × 10⁻⁶ and linear drift to (−1.36 ± 0.91) × 10⁻¹⁶ per year4. Most restrictively, Lange et al. (2021) compared the electric quadrupole (688.359 THz) and electric octupole (642.121 THz) transitions of ytterbium-171 ions—whose 4f-shell proximity to the heavy nucleus gives high sensitivity to the fine-structure constant—alongside cesium fountains, bounding temporal drift of the fine-structure constant to 1.0(1.1) × 10⁻¹⁸ per year, proton-to-electron mass ratio drift to −8(36) × 10⁻¹⁸ per year, and gravitational potential couplings to (14 ± 11\) × 10⁻⁹ for the fine-structure constant and (7 ± 45\) × 10⁻⁸ for the mass ratio3. Complementing these clock bounds, the BACON optical network demonstrated 18-digit ratio metrology among aluminum, ytterbium, and strontium standards65 (extended in a December 2025 preprint66), Sanner et al. tested directional anisotropy with ytterbium ions at 10⁻¹⁸ precision67, and the MICROSCOPE satellite constrained the Eötvös weak-equivalence parameter between titanium and platinum test masses to \[−1.5 ± 2.3 (statistical) ± 1.5 (systematic)\] × 10⁻¹⁵13. As Will (2014) demonstrates via energy conservation, any composition-dependent violation of clock-shift universality implies a corresponding equivalence-principle acceleration anomaly, directly linking MICROSCOPE's 10⁻¹⁵ null result to atomic-clock universality69.

## **Companion File 4: ADVOCACY\_SUMMARY.md (Plain-English ArcSecs Summary)**

Imagine two identical grandfather clocks: one sits in a basement, and the other sits at the top of a skyscraper, or flies around the world on an airplane. When brought back together, they no longer show the exact same number of ticks. Conventional physics explains this by saying that "time itself" stretched or slowed down for one of the clocks.  
The ArcSecs research program takes a simpler, physical-first starting point: time is just the "When"—the universal order in which events happen—and space is just the "Where." Neither is an invisible rubber sheet that can be stretched or bent. If an atomic clock at the top of the Tokyo Skytree ticks 21 cycles per second faster than an identical clock on the ground6, or if fast-moving muons in a particle ring survive nearly 30 times longer before decaying11, something physical inside those atoms and particles must be running at a different rate while universal time stays completely constant.  
Our quantitative review across over forty precision physics benchmarks shows two critical facts. First, if gravity and motion change the physical ticking and decay rates of all atoms and particles by the exact same universal percentage—what we call Model A—then the ArcSecs constant-time framework matches every modern measurement tested here with zero extra fudge factors (chi-squared 4.177 across 8 experiments)1. It fits millimeter-scale strontium clouds5, GPS satellite clocks49, lithium ion beams10, and high-energy muon storage rings11 just as accurately as conventional textbooks do.  
Second, honesty demands admitting what is still missing. Right now, Model A works mathematically, but we do not yet have a microscopic force equation explaining *why* gravity and speed alter electromagnetic electrons, nuclear protons, and weak muon decays by the exact same percentage down to twenty decimal places. To move beyond matching old numbers and discover something genuinely new, we propose watching the brand-new thorium-229 nuclear clock2 side-by-side with a strontium atomic clock for a full year as Earth orbits closer to and farther from the Sun. Because the thorium nucleus balances powerful strong-nuclear forces in a way no electron shell does72, any true physical-process difference will show up as a tiny annual wobble between the two clocks—giving science a clean, decisive test of whether constant universal time leaves a physical fingerprint in nature.

## ArcSecs interpretation-versus-observation checkpoint (editorial addition, R29)

- **Observed or calibrated quantities:** Frequency ratios, travel-time comparisons, and unstable-particle lifetimes are measured physical events.
- **What the observation does not determine by itself:** It does not identify a unique fundamental physical ontology or interaction mechanism; experimental limits must remain explicitly labeled as limits.
- **ArcSecs mechanism-building task:** Advance a physical-process account of those differences without describing time itself as material or experimentally stretchable.
- **Reproducible comparison / sources:** Atomic clocks, GPS clock networks, transverse Doppler spectroscopy, muon storage rings.
- **Falsification rule:** Retain every published measured quantity and uncertainty. If a specified ArcSecs mechanism fails that benchmark, record the failing assumption and design a more complete physical mechanism or a discriminating observation; do not erase or recast the measurement as a successful fit.
- **Scientific release status:** Research working edition. Source bibliography, numerical calculations, author attribution and any claimed original simulations require independent scientific verification before journal submission.
