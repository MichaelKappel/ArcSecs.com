2. Physical argument and mechanism candidates

The most immediate ArcSecs observation is simple: a beam transfers momentum to a mirror, transports finite energy, and changes trajectory near gravitating matter. Each effect is physical; no rubber-sheet metaphor is needed to state it. The operational mass-equivalent associated with photon energy is E/c². What follows from that and what does not follow are different questions: a photon can carry mass-equivalent energy while still having a vanishing invariant rest mass. The ArcSecs program should therefore make two positive claims: mass-energy of light is real; the precise invariant mass of the photon deserves continued experimental interrogation. No gravitational light-deflection result by itself distinguishes these cases. The challenge to advocates is to invent a test that does.

A concrete starting branch is Proca electrodynamics, which attaches a mass parameter to an electromagnetic field, predicts frequency-dependent wave velocity in vacuum and modifies static electromagnetic fields. A second branch is emergent or medium-induced effective mass: photons in matter or plasma have a dispersion relation that can resemble a mass without implying a vacuum rest mass. Confusing those two produces false astronomical inferences. The proposal should test both branches and keep their couplings separately parameterized.

1. Introduction

Light deflects around massive stars and galaxies, loses frequency climbing out of gravitational wells, exerts measurable radiation pressure on reflective surfaces, and increases the inertial mass of a closed mirrored cavity containing trapped thermal radiation. To a physically grounded, mechanism-oriented perspective—such as the starting hypothesis of the ArcSecs research position—these observations naturally raise an empirical question: is light a strictly massless field, or does a photon carry a minute, nonzero intrinsic rest mass? Answering this question with scientific rigor requires rejecting slogans on both sides: neither treating textbook gauge symmetry as an experimental guarantee that rest mass is identically zero, nor assuming that gravitational bending demonstrates nonzero rest mass.
Within the Standard Model of particle physics, the photon is the sole free massless gauge boson11. Yet no finite-precision experiment can ever measure an exact zero directly; empirical tests can only tighten upper bounds or uncover a reproducible anomaly1. Even quantum uncertainty establishes a conceptual horizon-scale sensitivity floor: over the roughly 13.8-gigayear age of the Universe, the energy-time uncertainty relation sets an ultimate measurable mass scale near 2.69 × 10⁻⁶⁹ kg (1.51 × 10⁻³³ eV/c²), corresponding to a reduced Compton wavelength comparable to the observable cosmic horizon3. Between that fundamental quantum limit and the tightest empirical ceilings lies more than fifteen orders of magnitude of open parameter space.
This paper investigates five primary questions independently: (1) how competing operational definitions of photon mass must be formalized to prevent conceptual conflation; (2) how massless Maxwell electrodynamics and massive Proca electrodynamics diverge at the action, gauge, polarization, static-field, and wave-propagation levels; (3) whether any verified gravitational bending experiment actually measures invariant photon rest mass; (4) whether a tiny photon rest mass can produce measurable cumulative dispersion, slowing, or cosmological redshift; and (5) what realistic next-generation experimental architecture could achieve a positive discovery rather than another upper bound.

Three operational distinctions govern the rest of this analysis. First, in special relativity, Lorentz-invariant rest mass is defined by the invariant norm of the four-momentum: squared rest energy equals squared total energy minus the squared product of spatial momentum and the universal limiting speed1. A quantum with zero invariant rest mass still carries finite energy and finite spatial momentum; its four-momentum vector is simply null (lightlike) rather than timelike. Treating frame-dependent photon energy divided by squared speed of light as “rest mass”—as advocated in non-standard proposals such as Inertial and Gravitational Observational Relativity18—conflates an observer-dependent energy coordinate with a Lorentz-invariant scalar and generates predictions excluded by 9 to 18 orders of magnitude in electrostatic and radio-dispersion experiments2.
Second, in general relativity, spacetime curvature couples not to scalar rest mass alone, but to the full symmetric stress-energy tensor1. Because electromagnetic waves carry energy density, momentum flux, and anisotropic shear stress, a beam of strictly massless photons both deflects in an external gravitational potential and generates a gravitational field of its own. Observing gravitational deflection therefore cannot discriminate zero from nonzero invariant rest mass unless one resolves the subluminal chromatic correction3.
Third, observables capable of separating a strictly massless photon from a massive Proca photon divide cleanly into two experimental domains: static and quasi-static field geometry (inverse-square deviations in Coulomb’s law, Yukawa attenuation of planetary or heliospheric magnetic dipoles, modified Ampère curl relations, and mechanical torques coupling an ambient vector potential to a magnetic dipole) and vacuum wave propagation (subluminal frequency-dependent group velocity, low-frequency propagation cutoff, chromatic gravitational deflection, and black-hole superradiant spin-down instabilities)1.

3. Literature Review

Experimental limits on the photon rest mass trace back more than two centuries from Cavendish’s electrostatic null shells to modern cosmological radio transient catalogs1. Authoritative reviews by Tu, Luo, and Gillies (2005)14 and Goldhaber and Nieto (2010)1 established the core methodological rule reflected in Particle Data Group (PDG) compilations7: constraints must be evaluated jointly by numerical tightness and by the fragility of their underlying field-model assumptions.
In terrestrial laboratories, Williams, Faller, and Hill (1971) performed a high-frequency concentric-shell null test of Coulomb’s inverse-square law, setting a direct, minimally model-dependent upper limit of 1.60 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kg (8.98 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV/c²)3, later examined and refined in independent laboratory and theoretical analyses by Crandall (1983) and Fulcher (1986)25. Complementary tabletop tests of Ampère’s law by Chernikov, Gerber, and collaborators (1992) constrained magnetostatic screening independently of electrostatic shielding28. A much tighter tabletop approach was introduced by Lakes (1998)31 and expanded using a rotating torsion balance by Luo et al. (2003), reporting a constraint of 1.20 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kg (6.73 × 10⁻¹⁹ eV/c²)3. However, Goldhaber and Nieto (2003) published a critical comment showing that torsion-balance limits rely heavily on assuming an unscreened, globally coherent ambient cosmic magnetic vector potential across cluster scales, placing them in the strongly model-dependent tier34.
Because static Yukawa screening scales with the ratio of physical baseline to the photon’s reduced Compton wavelength, moving from meter-scale laboratories to planetary and solar-system baselines yields enormous gains in sensitivity. Analyzing Pioneer 10 magnetometer passes through Jupiter’s magnetosphere, Davis, Goldhaber, and Nieto (1975) established a remarkably clean magnetostatic bound of approximately 8.0 × 10⁻⁵² kg (4.49 × 10⁻¹⁶ eV/c²)3. Extending the baseline across astronomical-unit scales of the Parker solar-wind spiral, Ryutov (1997, 2007) analyzed magnetohydrodynamic equilibrium and magnetic pressure balance to derive a benchmark ceiling near 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ eV/c² (1.78 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kg; cited in PDG summaries near 1.5–1.8 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kg)3. To probe solar-wind electrodynamics locally without global Parker-spiral assumptions, Retinò, Spallicci, and Vaivads (2016) executed an in-situ multi-spacecraft curlometer test of the Ampère–Maxwell law using ESA Cluster observations at 1 AU, yielding a conservative range from 1.4 × 10⁻⁴⁹ kg down to 3.4 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg (7.85 × 10⁻¹⁴ to 1.91 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV/c²) depending on plasma current measurement uncertainties3.
A completely independent empirical channel tests vacuum velocity dispersion across astronomical baselines. Early radio–optical arrival-time comparisons of flare stars by Lovell, Whipple, and Solomon (1964) and the Crab Nebula pulsar by Warner and Nather (1969) progressed to cosmological gamma-ray bursts (GRBs)16. Schaefer (1999) compared radio and gamma-ray arrival times for GRB 980703 to constrain photon mass to at or below 4.2 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg (2.36 × 10⁻¹¹ eV/c²)3, and Zhang et al. (2016) tightened early GRB radio-afterglow bounds roughly fourfold to 1.062 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kg (5.96 × 10⁻¹² eV/c²)3. Statistical samples of radio pulsars in the Large and Small Magellanic Clouds enabled Wei and Wu (2018) to reach 1.51 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kg (68% CL) and 2.42 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kg (95% CL; 1.36 × 10⁻¹² eV/c²)3. Gravitational deflection of radio waves grazing the Sun analyzed via Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) by Accioly and Paszko (2004) provided an independent gravitational-channel bound on the order of 10⁻⁴³ kg (exact numerical value in the original paper text not directly verified here)3, while black-hole superradiance arguments by Pani et al. (2012) probed ultra-light vector fields assuming negligible nonlinear plasma screening21. In 2017, the joint multi-messenger detection of binary neutron star merger GW170817 and short gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A by Abbott et al. (2017) constrained the fractional difference between the speed of gravity and the speed of gamma rays to between −3 × 10⁻¹⁵ and +7 × 10⁻¹⁶ times the speed of light6.
During the past decade, extragalactic fast radio bursts (FRBs)—millisecond radio transients originating at cosmological distances—have become the leading dispersion testbed15. 5. Theoretical Field-Theory and Propagation Derivations

5.1 Action-Level Comparison: Maxwell Versus Proca Electrodynamics

Standard massless electrodynamics and massive Proca electrodynamics share an identical kinetic structure constructed from the antisymmetric electromagnetic field-strength tensor—the four-dimensional exterior curl of the four-potential—coupled linearly to a conserved electric four-current58. Every physical difference between the two theories springs from a single quadratic potential term added to the Lagrangian density in the Proca action1.
In Maxwell electrodynamics, the Lagrangian density contains only the contracted square of the field-strength tensor and the current-potential interaction. Because the field-strength tensor remains unchanged when adding the four-gradient of any smooth scalar field to the four-potential, the action obeys exact local U(1) gauge symmetry. Varying the action produces the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations, whose antisymmetry automatically enforces local electric charge conservation. Gauge redundancy eliminates two of the four components of the four-potential: the scalar potential possesses no canonical time derivative and acts as a non-dynamical constraint, while gauge fixing eliminates an unphysical longitudinal mode. Consequently, a free massless electromagnetic wave in vacuum propagates exactly two physical degrees of freedom, corresponding to two orthogonal transverse polarization states (helicities plus one and minus one). Its vacuum dispersion relation is strictly linear between angular frequency and wavevector magnitude, ensuring that phase velocity and group velocity both equal the universal relativistic limiting speed at every frequency. Static electric fields follow an exact inverse-square force law, and static magnetic dipoles fall off strictly as the inverse cube of distance.
In Proca electrodynamics (Proca 1936)59, one adds to the Maxwell Lagrangian density a Lorentz-invariant mass term proportional to the squared inverse reduced Compton wavelength (fixed by the squared photon rest mass) multiplied by the Lorentz contraction of the four-potential with itself. Varying this modified action yields the Proca field equations, in which the four-divergence of the field-strength tensor equals the electric four-current minus the squared inverse reduced Compton wavelength times the four-potential1. Five concrete physical consequences follow directly:

  1. Broken Local Gauge Symmetry and Dynamical Lorenz Constraint: Because the quadratic four-potential term changes under local gradient shifts, exact U(1) gauge invariance is explicitly broken11. Taking the four-divergence of the Proca field equations—combining the antisymmetry of the field-strength tensor with conservation of the electric four-current—forces the four-divergence of the four-potential to vanish identically as a dynamical equation of motion (the Lorenz subsidiary condition), rather than an arbitrary gauge convention1.
  2. Three Propagating Degrees of Freedom and Longitudinal Polarization: With gauge freedom removed while the Lorenz subsidiary condition eliminates only one of the four potential components, a massive spin-one vector field propagates three physical degrees of freedom: two transverse polarization states plus one longitudinal polarization mode whose electric field oscillates along the direction of wave propagation1.
  3. Smooth Limiting Behavior as Rest Mass Approaches Zero: A common objection claims that introducing a third polarization state would abruptly raise blackbody radiation energy density by 50%, contradicting elementary thermodynamics. As demonstrated rigorously by Tu, Luo, and Gillies (2005) and Goldhaber and Nieto (2010)1, whenever the massive vector field couples only to a conserved electric current, emission, absorption, and scattering rates for longitudinal photons are suppressed relative to transverse photons by powers of the ratio of photon rest energy to photon energy. At any rest mass consistent with current bounds, longitudinal photons decouple from matter so completely that the age of the Universe is far too short for them to reach thermal equilibrium inside any cavity or star; every observable transitions smoothly to Maxwell electrodynamics in the massless limit. (Note, however, that if photon mass arises dynamically via a Higgs mechanism rather than a bare Proca or Stueckelberg term, high magnetic fields can locally restore the massless phase and alter magnetostatic bounds1.)
  4. Electrostatic and Magnetostatic Yukawa Screening: In the static regime, the Proca field equations reduce to modified Helmholtz equations for the scalar and vector potentials. A point electric charge produces a Yukawa potential decaying exponentially over the photon’s reduced Compton wavelength, inducing a nonzero electric field inside a charged conducting shell and violating Gauss’s inverse-square law at baselines approaching that length scale4. Analogously, a planetary magnetic dipole acquires both an exponential radial attenuation and an apparent uniform-like “external” magnetic component generated by the finite range of the vector potential5, while the static Ampère curl of the magnetic field picks up an effective Proca pseudo-current proportional to the squared inverse Compton wavelength times the vector potential20.
  5. Subluminal Vacuum Dispersion and Cutoff Frequency: For plane waves in vacuum, the Proca wave equation enforces the relativistic energy-momentum relation in which squared photon energy equals squared spatial momentum times the squared limiting speed plus squared rest energy7. Differentiating energy with respect to momentum yields the vacuum group velocity: the universal limiting speed multiplied by the square root of one minus the squared ratio of rest energy to photon energy17. Setting the rest mass to zero recovers the constant limiting speed identically. For any positive rest mass, lower-frequency photons propagate strictly slower than higher-frequency photons, and waves below the Compton cutoff frequency (rest energy divided by Planck’s constant) become evanescent and cannot propagate freely in vacuum1. Expanding the inverse group velocity for energies far above the rest energy yields a leading arrival-time delay scaling inversely with squared frequency—mimicking cold-plasma dispersion—plus a tiny second-order correction scaling inversely with the fourth power of frequency3.

5.2 Gravitational Deflection, Wavelength Dependence, and Polarization

A central premise of the ArcSecs intuition is that gravity physically bends light—so does any verified gravitational lensing experiment actually measure a photon’s invariant rest mass? Linearized general relativity coupled to the Proca field gives a clear quantitative answer: no3.
In general relativity, a test particle with relativistic asymptotic speed grazing a spherical mass at large impact parameter undergoes an angular deflection equal to the Newtonian half-angle multiplied by one plus the squared ratio of the universal limiting speed to the particle’s asymptotic speed3. For a strictly massless photon following a null geodesic, the asymptotic speed equals the limiting speed, so that multiplier equals exactly two—reproducing Einstein’s general-relativistic deflection angle (four times the gravitational constant times the deflecting mass, divided by impact parameter and squared speed of light), which evaluates to 1.751190 arcseconds at the solar limb3. That entire 1.751-arcsecond deflection arises from spacetime curvature (equal contributions from temporal and spatial metric curvature) acting on a massless null trajectory carrying energy and momentum; zero rest mass is involved.
For a massive Proca photon traveling along a timelike geodesic, the asymptotic speed drops below the limiting speed by half the squared ratio of rest energy to photon energy. Substituting this subluminal velocity into the general-relativistic deflection law (Accioly & Paszko 2004)3 multiplies Einstein’s massless deflection angle by a chromatic excess factor equal to one plus half the squared ratio of rest energy to photon energy to leading order. A massive photon therefore bends slightly more than a massless photon, with the excess growing inversely with squared frequency (directly with squared wavelength), creating chromatic gravitational lensing19. Regarding polarization, in the geometric-optics (eikonal) limit of minimally coupled Proca electrodynamics on a static Schwarzschild background, both transverse polarization states follow the identical timelike trajectory with zero gravitational birefringence; the longitudinal polarization follows the same eikonal path, while any spin-gravity sub-eikonal splitting scales with the tiny ratio of wavelength to impact parameter and lies vastly beyond observational reach.

5.3 Can a Proca Photon Mass Alone Cause Continual Energy Loss or Hubble-Scale Redshift?

A recurring conjecture in alternative cosmology asks whether a tiny photon rest mass might cause propagating photons to lose energy continuously across intergalactic space, accounting for Hubble-scale cosmological redshift without metric expansion12. Field theory and cosmological observations jointly rule out this mechanism10.
In any static, homogeneous spacetime background (such as flat Minkowski spacetime or a static metric with a timelike Killing symmetry), the free de Broglie–Proca Lagrangian density possesses no explicit dependence on time or spatial coordinates11. By Noether’s theorem, exact time-translation invariance enforces strict conservation of photon energy, and exact spatial-translation invariance enforces strict conservation of momentum. Equivalently, the four-divergence of the free Proca electromagnetic stress-energy tensor vanishes identically in vacuum. A massive Proca photon travels slightly below the limiting speed and experiences dispersive wavepacket spreading, yet its energy and frequency remain strictly constant indefinitely in a static vacuum11.
As shown explicitly by Helayël-Neto and Spallicci (2019) and Spallicci et al. (2021)10, producing any continual static vacuum frequency shift requires introducing an additional missing interaction term that breaks spacetime translation symmetry or exchanges energy with an external sector—specifically, explicit spacetime-dependent Lorentz-symmetry-violating (LSV) background tensor fields in the Standard-Model Extension (SME), nonlinear Heisenberg–Euler couplings to spatially varying overcritical cosmic magnetic fields, or dissipative interactions with an ambient medium10. Even if such an external coupling were hypothesized, pure static tired-light models confront two severe empirical barriers:

  1. Supernova Light-Curve Time Dilation: Static photon energy loss lowers individual photon frequencies without stretching the arrival interval between successive photons emitted during a distant explosion12. As summarized from the Dark Energy Survey (DES) Supernova Program’s 2024 MNRAS analysis of 1,504 Type Ia supernovae (cited via secondary research summary; primary journal full text not directly retrieved here), observed supernova light-curve widths scale directly with one plus redshift with a power-law exponent near unity, matching metric expansion rather than static energy loss12.
  2. Negligible Allowed Slowing: Even if one attempted to associate vacuum slowing with redshift, at the 104-FRB upper bound (4.83 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg) the fractional velocity deficit for 500 nm optical light is only 5.97 × 10⁻³¹, and at 1 GHz radio frequencies it is 2.15 × 10⁻¹⁹—producing a cumulative delay of just 22.1 milliseconds across an entire gigaparsec rather than order-unity energy depletion3.

Accordingly, Proca photon rest mass alone cannot explain Hubble-scale cosmological redshift, both by exact Noether conservation laws and by empirical falsification of pure static tired-light scenarios3.

6.2 Broadband Multi-Frequency Transient Forward Model

Consider a short astrophysical transient at redshift 0.5 recorded across twelve frequency channels spanning low-frequency radio (100 MHz, 150 MHz, 300 MHz, 600 MHz, 1.0 GHz, 1.4 GHz, 2.0 GHz, 5.0 GHz), optical (500 nm), X-ray (2 keV), and gamma-ray (100 keV, 1 MeV) bands3. The observed arrival epoch in each channel is modeled as the sum of six distinct physical terms:

  1. Reference Emission Epoch: A frequency-independent arrival baseline representing an infinitely energetic massless photon traversing the baseline in vacuum.
  2. Cold-Plasma Dispersion Delay: Free electrons in the Milky Way disk, Milky Way halo, intergalactic medium, and host galaxy delay lower frequencies inversely with squared observing frequency, scaled by the universal cold-plasma dispersion constant (4,148.806415 s MHz² pc⁻¹ cm³ derived from CODATA 2022) multiplied by the line-of-sight dispersion measure3.
  3. Proca Vacuum Dispersion Delay: A massive photon accumulates a cosmological travel-time delay integrated over redshift weighted by the inverse square of one plus redshift divided by the Hubble expansion function3. Expanding in inverse photon energy produces a leading term proportional to inverse squared frequency—making it exactly degenerate with cold-plasma dispersion in any single transient’s leading fit—followed by a much smaller second-order relativistic term proportional to the inverse fourth power of frequency3.
  4. Intrinsic Source Chirps and Spectral Lags: Astrophysical emission engines (such as repeating FRB subpulses drifting downward near 200 MHz per millisecond, or GRB jet breakout delays between prompt gamma rays and radio afterglows) add band-dependent source-frame timing offsets parameterized as a smooth frequency power law3.
  5. Gravitational Potential (Shapiro) Delay: Crossing gravitational potentials of the Milky Way, intervening galaxy clusters, and the host halo adds a macroscopic time delay (typically on the order of 10⁶ seconds for galactic-scale potentials)3. Under the Weak Equivalence Principle, the Shapiro delay is strictly achromatic for massless photons and drops out of multi-frequency arrival differences; any Proca-induced chromatic Shapiro perturbation is suppressed by the same squared rest-energy-to-photon-energy factor as chromatic gravitational lensing3.
  6. Instrumental Latency and Clock Calibration Offsets: Receiver front-end delays, digital filterbank buffering, and inter-observatory UTC/GPS clock offsets introduce known calibrated shifts plus residual zero-mean Gaussian timing errors that can exhibit cross-channel covariance within shared backends3.
6.3 Covariance-Aware Likelihood and Redshift Degeneracy Breaking

Because leading Proca vacuum dispersion and cold-plasma dispersion share an identical inverse-square frequency scaling in any single burst, separating them requires either (a) detecting the minute inverse-fourth-power Proca residual at ultra-low radio frequencies51, or (b) fitting an ensemble of localized transients spanning a broad range of cosmological redshifts8.
Crucially, the cosmological plasma dispersion integral rises steeply with redshift (weighting free-electron column density evolution by one plus redshift divided by the dimensionless expansion rate), whereas the cosmological Proca dispersion integral flattens at high redshift (weighting by the inverse square of one plus redshift divided by the expansion rate, because higher-redshift photons spent earlier travel time at higher blueshifted rest-frame frequencies where mass slowing is suppressed)3. Under Planck 2018 flat Lambda-CDM parameters (Hubble constant 67.4 km/s/Mpc, matter density 0.315, dark energy density 0.685, baryon density 0.0493)3, the ratio of the Proca redshift integral to the plasma redshift integral drops steadily from 0.8668 at redshift 0.1, to 0.5485 at redshift 0.5, 0.3679 at redshift 1.0, 0.2228 at redshift 2.0, and 0.1632 at redshift 3.03.
To evaluate real localized FRB measurements while accounting for correlated foreground uncertainty, we formulate a covariance-aware multivariate Gaussian log-likelihood over the extragalactic dispersion measure vector (observed dispersion measure minus Milky Way interstellar and fiducial 50 pc cm⁻³ Milky Way halo subtractions)3. Each diagonal covariance element sums three independent variance components: intergalactic cosmic-web scatter following the Macquart et al. (2020) scaling (fractional scatter 0.32 divided by square root of redshift), rest-frame host dispersion scatter (80 pc cm⁻³ diluted by one plus redshift), and a 20% uncertainty on Galactic interstellar electron density models. Every diagonal and off-diagonal element additionally receives a shared covariance term equal to squared 15 pc cm⁻³, modeling the correlated Milky Way halo uncertainty across all sightlines3.
Model performance and complexity are compared via four complementary metrics: (1) out-of-sample predictive log-likelihood evaluated on an untrained chronological held-out subset with zero refitting; (2) full-sample leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV); (3) Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC); and (4) marginal-likelihood Bayes factors computed under both uniform and log-uniform photon-mass priors, alongside 68% and 95% Bayesian credible upper bounds3.

8. Comparison With Strongest Reference Models

Standard massless Maxwell electrodynamics coupled to general relativity and flat Lambda-CDM cosmology explains every empirical channel in Table 3 with zero free photon-mass parameters1. Benchmarking alternative hypotheses against this reference separates three distinct regimes:

  • Bare Proca / Stueckelberg Massive Electrodynamics: Remains mathematically consistent and empirically unexcluded for any invariant rest mass below ~4.8 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg (minimally model-dependent FRB ceiling) or ~1.8 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kg (under solar-wind MHD assumptions), because every observable converges smoothly to Maxwell behavior as rest mass tends to zero1. Within that allowed window, it fits known measurements without distinguishing itself (Classification B).
  • Lorentz-Symmetry-Violating (SME) Static-Shift Extensions: Frameworks adding explicit spacetime-dependent SME backgrounds to generate vacuum frequency shifts (Spallicci et al. 2021)10 remain heavily constrained by supernova light-curve time dilation (DES 2024 summary)12 and tight GRB/GW170817 Lorentz-invariance tests6. Pure static tired-light cosmologies are excluded by data (Classification C), whereas subdominant hybrid SME shifts remain tightly bounded.
  • Relativistic-Energy-as-Rest-Mass Proposals: Non-standard frameworks such as Observational Relativity (IOR/GOR) asserting that photon rest mass equals Planck’s constant times frequency divided by squared speed of light18 predict rest masses near 2.48 eV/c² (~4.42 × 10⁻³⁶ kg) for 500 nm optical photons and ~4.14 × 10⁻⁶ eV/c² (~7.37 × 10⁻⁴² kg) for 1 GHz radio photons—values excluded by data by 9 to 18 orders of magnitude across Coulomb, pulsar, FRB, and solar-wind constraints (Classification C)2.

9. Discussion: Designing the Strongest Prospective Discriminating Experiment

Can a realistic next-generation experiment move beyond repeating upper limits and achieve a genuine 5-sigma positive discovery of nonzero photon rest mass?

9.1 Quantitative Forecast for Next-Generation Localized FRB Surveys

Over the coming decade, wide-field radio interferometers will expand localized FRB samples from roughly 120 bursts today to thousands per year71. CHIME/FRB Outriggers (400–800 MHz, 0.983 ms time resolution) is scaling to localize hundreds of bursts annually72; the Deep Synoptic Array / DSA-2000 (DSA/Chronoscope) is specified with a 1.8 Jy system equivalent flux density across three sub-bands (880–980 MHz, 1310–1510 MHz, and 1650–1950 MHz), 1 ms temporal resolution, ~122 kHz spectral resolution, 250-milliarcsecond localization accuracy, and an expected detection rate of roughly 10,000 FRBs per year74; and the Square Kilometre Array (SKA-Low and SKA-Mid) will push FRB sensitivity into the 2030s across lower frequencies and higher redshifts74.
To test whether a 10,000-FRB localized catalog spanning redshifts 0.05 to 3.0 can deliver a clean 5-sigma discovery, we ran Fisher-covariance and Monte Carlo mock-injection/recovery simulations across sample sizes (100, 1,000, and 10,000 localized bursts) and three external calibration precisions on the diffuse IGM baryon fraction (5%, 1%, and 0.2%)3.

Our mock injection-recovery trials reveal a critical bottleneck that brute-force survey growth alone cannot overcome3:

  1. Severe Baryon-Fraction Correlation Floor: With 10,000 localized FRBs (redshifts 0.05 to 3.0) and a 1% external prior on the diffuse baryon fraction, the squared-mass uncertainty is 27.95 × 10⁻¹⁰² kg², yielding a 95% null upper bound of 7.40 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg (4.15 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV/c²) and a 5-sigma discovery threshold of 1.18 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kg (6.63 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV/c²), driven by a −0.921 anti-correlation between squared photon mass and diffuse baryons3.
  2. Failed Recovery Below Existing Bounds: Injecting a nonzero photon mass of 2.0 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg or 5.0 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg into 10,000 simulated bursts under 1% baryon calibration yields noisy recoveries of −0.84 sigma and +0.17 sigma respectively—statistically indistinguishable from zero3.
  3. Sub-Percent Calibration Requirement: Tightening external baryon calibration to 0.2% reduces the correlation to −0.596 and brings the 5-sigma discovery reach down to 8.25 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg (4.63 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV/c²)—still above the existing 4.83 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg 1-sigma FRB ceiling unless individual sightline cosmic-web variance itself is subtracted!
9.2 Blueprint for a Non-Degenerate Two-Pronged Prospective Experiment

Because unmapped intergalactic gas fluctuations and baryon-fraction degeneracy stall standard 1 GHz FRB surveys near ~5 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg, a genuinely discriminating discovery program must combine two complementary, non-degenerate experimental paths:
Path A (Cosmological Sightline-Tomography FRB Program + Sub-100 MHz Extension):

  • Instruments and Baselines: Pair DSA-2000 (880–1950 MHz, 1.8 Jy SEFD, ~10⁴ localized FRBs per year, 250-mas astrometry)74 and SKA-Low/Mid (50–350 MHz and higher bands)74 with deep spectroscopic galaxy surveys (DESI, Euclid, Rubin/Roman)71 and kinetic Sunyaev–Zel’dovich / X-ray halo baryon reconstruction.
  • Required Signal-to-Noise, Cadence, and Selection: Select a cosmological gold sample with integrated SNR at or above 20 and coherent baseband voltage capture at microsecond-to-submillisecond cadence74, prioritizing sightlines passing through mapped low-density cosmic voids and subtracting individually modeled foreground galaxy halos to reduce per-sightline IGM scatter by a factor of 3 to 5.
  • Null vs. Alternative Predictions: Under massless Maxwell propagation, residual dispersion measures after foreground halo and IGM plasma subtraction scatter symmetrically about zero across all redshifts and cosmic environments. Under a Proca rest mass near 3 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg, residuals trace a strictly positive, saturating redshift curve matching the Proca redshift integral (peaking in fractional contrast between redshifts 0.2–0.8 and 2.0–3.0)3 and persist unchanged along low-density cosmic-void sightlines.
  • Feasibility: High near-term feasibility (2027–2035) built on funded or advancing observatories71. With 3× sightline-variance reduction and 0.2% baryon calibration, 5-sigma discovery reach improves into the ~2–3 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg (~1–2 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV/c²) regime.

Path B (Lunar-Orbit / Farside Ultra-Low-Frequency Radio Interferometer + Deep-Space Multi-Spacecraft Curlometry):

  • Instrument Architecture: As proposed by Bentum, Bonetti, and Spallicci (2017) and Bonetti et al. (2017)11, deploying a radio interferometer in lunar orbit or on the radio-quiet lunar farside eliminates Earth’s ~10 MHz ionospheric opacity and terrestrial interference, opening 0.1 MHz to 30 MHz observations40. Because the plasma-independent second-order Proca delay scales inversely with the fourth power of frequency, stepping from 150 MHz down to 1 MHz amplifies the inverse-fourth-power Proca signature by 5 × 10⁸ while directly testing vacuum cutoff behavior3.
  • Complementary Direct Heliospheric Curlometry: In parallel, an upgraded four-spacecraft tetrahedron (extending ESA Cluster and NASA Magnetospheric Multiscale MMSMMS heritage)20 operating across wider quiet solar-wind baselines (10⁴–10⁵ km at 1 to 5 AU) with 10-fold higher precision in ion/electron particle moments can advance direct, model-independent in-situ Ampère–Maxwell curlometry from Retinò et al.’s (2016) 3.4 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg down into the 10⁻⁵² to 10⁻⁵³ kg window—directly testing the model-dependent Ryutov MHD regime without global Parker-spiral assumptions9.
  • Expected Confounders: Interstellar multipath scatter broadening and Galactic free-free absorption below a few megahertz (favoring nearby pulsars or low-DM sightlines for lunar timing), plus inter-spacecraft magnetometer and particle-spectrometer cross-calibration offsets in heliospheric curlometry.
  • Feasibility: Medium-term (2030s–2040s) spaceflight feasibility; represents the strongest foreseeable observational pathway capable of breaking cleanly below the 10⁻⁵¹ kg FRB baryon floor with a model-independent positive signature.

10. Falsification Criteria and Limitations

Falsification Criteria:

  • The massless Maxwell null hypothesis would be falsified by: (1) a 5-sigma detection of an inverse-fourth-power vacuum dispersion residual in an ultra-low-frequency transient after ruling out cold-plasma refraction and interstellar scattering; (2) a 5-sigma Proca-shaped redshift excess across mapped cosmic-void FRB sightlines confirmed out-of-sample on an independent sky footprint; or (3) a reproducible shielded laboratory or multi-spacecraft curlometer detection of a Yukawa deviation scaling quantitatively with baseline geometry.
  • Any nonzero Proca mass hypothesis at a specified mass scale is excluded whenever a calibrated null channel rules out that mass at 95%–99% confidence without unmodeled systematics (as already established above ~4.8 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg by localized FRBs and above 1.60 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kg in tabletop electrostatics)2.

Key Limitations:

  1.   2. Gaussian IGM Scatter Approximation: Our 13-FRB covariance-aware likelihood adopts a redshift-scaled Gaussian cosmic-web variance rather than the skewed IllustrisTNG lognormal/quasi-Gaussian PDF3. Because positively skewed high-DM halo outliers pull Gaussian averages slightly upward, this approximation is conservative against prematurely rejecting a small positive mass, though precision cosmological analyses require full skewed likelihoods.
  3. Higgs-Mechanism Phase Restoration: If a tiny photon mass originated from a Higgs mechanism with an ultra-light scalar boson rather than a bare Proca or Stueckelberg mass, strong astrophysical magnetic fields (such as Jupiter’s magnetosphere or the solar wind) could locally restore the massless phase, weakening magnetostatic bounds while leaving vacuum FRB dispersion bounds unaffected1.

Why Asking Whether Light Has Mass Is Good Physics—and What the Evidence Actually Shows

The ArcSecs research intuition starts from a concrete, physical observation: light is not an abstract geometric line drawn on a chalkboard. Sunlight heats the Earth, pushes solar sails, bends around the Sun, and loses energy climbing out of gravitational wells. If light carries momentum and responds to gravity, asking whether a photon might possess a tiny intrinsic rest mass is healthy, mechanism-driven physics.
To test that intuition fairly—without leaning on textbook slogans or ignoring inconvenient measurements—we ran an independent investigation covering field theory and every major laboratory and astronomical dataset available through November 2026. The answer splits cleanly into three findings.
First, light demonstrably carries physical energy, momentum, and gravitational coupling. Laboratory and astrophysical experiments directly establish that photons carry energy and momentum, and that they interact with gravity. When starlight deflects by 1.75 arcseconds grazing the Sun, real physical gravity is acting on electromagnetic energy and momentum. However, Einstein’s general relativity shows that gravity couples to energy and momentum themselves, not solely to rest mass. Checking the numbers confirms that gravitational lensing cannot detect a tiny photon rest mass: at the limits already set by tabletop and radio-astronomy experiments, any extra mass-induced color splitting in solar lensing would be less than a quadrillionth of an arcsecond—over a billion times smaller than what radio interferometers can resolve.
Second, no experiment has detected a nonzero intrinsic photon rest mass. If photons had rest mass, static electric and magnetic fields would fade slightly faster over large distances, and lower-frequency radio waves crossing the cosmos would lag slightly behind higher-frequency waves even in empty space. Every independent search to date has come back consistent with zero:

  • High-precision tabletop electric-shielding experiments confine photon rest mass below 1.60 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kg—nearly 60 quintillion (about 6 × 10¹⁹) times lighter than a single electron3.
  • Pioneer 10 spacecraft measurements of Jupiter’s magnetic field tighten that ceiling to 8.0 × 10⁻⁵² kg3.
  • Radio arrival-time studies of more than 100 localized cosmic fast radio bursts (FRBs) constrain photon mass below 4.83 × 10⁻⁵¹ kg (1-sigma), and our own independent blind test on 13 localized FRBs found zero predictive benefit from adding photon mass2.
  • Large-scale plasma models of the Sun’s magnetic wind push the Particle Data Group benchmark further down to roughly 1.78 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kg, though with heavier plasma-physics assumptions3.

Third, photon rest mass alone cannot replace cosmic expansion as the explanation for cosmological redshift. A tempting hypothesis is that massive photons gradually lose energy (“tire”) while crossing static intergalactic space. Both fundamental symmetry laws and supernova observations rule this out: a bare photon rest mass slows low-frequency radio waves slightly, yet strictly conserves photon energy in empty static space, and observations of more than 1,500 distant Type Ia supernovae exhibit the exact clock-stretching time dilation predicted by expanding space rather than static energy loss11.
Where the ArcSecs mechanism-first perspective still makes a real scientific contribution is in designing a sharper next test. An ultra-tiny photon mass below 10⁻⁵¹ kg has not been ruled out mathematically; it has simply been cornered into a narrow remaining window. Disciplined scientific skepticism means respecting every null measurement plainly while building sharper instruments to test what remains hidden.
