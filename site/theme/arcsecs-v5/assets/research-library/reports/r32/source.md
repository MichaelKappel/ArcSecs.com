## **Research Questions and Operational Definitions**

This study resolves five foundational questions: (1) what "age of the universe" operationally means in cyclic, finite-beginning, or past-eternal cosmologies, distinguishing an observable hot phase from total physical reality and individual stellar lifetimes; (2) which empirical methods establish strict minimum ages versus partly independent or globally model-dependent ages; (3) how measured angles, fluxes, colors, spectra, parallaxes, and proper motions map onto cataloged distances; (4) whether extreme z \> 20 candidates can be nearby substellar objects, how often such false positives occur, and what they imply—and do not imply—about confirmed z ≈ 14 galaxies; and (5) how competing cosmologies bound cosmic age across explicit model families without assuming a disputed redshift mechanism.  
Operationally, the universal progression parameter T denotes the constant ordering coordinate ("When") along which physical interactions, emissions, and detections take place. A chronometric age measures the duration in constant T required for a physical system—such as a main-sequence turn-off star, a crystallizing white dwarf, or an actinide nucleus—to evolve from an initial physical boundary state to its observed state2. In finite-beginning expanding benchmarks, the global age parameter t0 integrates an assumed expansion rate back to a formal density singularity3; in cyclic or past-eternal models—including the static-Euclidean ArcSecs framework—total cosmic duration Tcosmos is unbounded above by local expansion rates and constrained only from below by surviving structures.  
Distance and redshift terms likewise require strict operational separation. A light-year is a legitimate Euclidean length unit ("Where") defined by multiplying a local electromagnetic speed by one Julian year, and must not be conflated with the model-dependent assumption that an extragalactic source's distance in light-years equals its light-travel duration under varying photon speed or geometry. A photometric redshift is a statistical template fit to multi-band flux dropouts7, whereas a spectroscopic redshift measures discrete wavelength shifts of identified atomic or molecular transitions8; mapping either shift onto distance requires a propagation theory. Proper motion measures apparent angular displacement across the sky in milliarcseconds per year (mas/yr) relative to background reference frames5. Every conclusion in this paper is classified as an observation, derived mathematics, model-dependent inference, hypothesis, or philosophical interpretation, and scored against four outcomes: (A) supports a new measurable prediction, (B) fits known measurements without distinguishing itself, (C) is excluded by data, or (D) remains undecidable.

## **Literature Review**

### **Stellar Evolution, White-Dwarf Cooling, and Radiochronometry**

Milky Way globular clusters provide the tightest empirical lower bounds on Galactic matter age when anchored to trigonometric parallaxes2. Using full color-magnitude diagram (CMD) Gaussian-mixture Bayesian hierarchical modeling of Hubble Space Telescope (HST) photometry across Galactic globular clusters, Valcin et al. (2021) reduced convective mixing-length uncertainties by roughly 50%, obtaining an inferred cosmic age of 13.5 \+0.16/−0.14 (stat.) ± 0.23 (0.33) (sys.) Gyr1. In Part III of that program (JCAP 2025(10):030), promoting initial helium fraction Y (0.2–0.4) and the dust reddening law RV (1.5–5) to free parameters alongside Gaia distance priors yielded an oldest cluster age of 13.39 ± 0.10 (stat.) ± 0.23 (sys.) Gyr, an inferred cosmic age (accounting for cluster formation delay) of 13.57 \+0.16/−0.14 (stat.) ± 0.23 (sys.) Gyr, and a 95% confidence upper limit of 13.92 \+0.13/−0.10 (stat.) ± 0.23 (sys.) Gyr2. Independent analyses of M92—moving from an earlier 11 ± 1.5 Gyr estimate19 to within one sigma of the 13.3–13.5 Gyr baseline4—and large Galactic old-star samples studied by Banik et al. (2026) confirm that statistical errors are now smaller than nuclear reaction-rate, opacity, and stellar-rotation systematics4. Notably, a 2021 MNRAS study of Gaia globular-cluster astrometry demonstrated that formal catalog uncertainties on parallax and proper motion in dense cores are underestimated by 10–20% (scaling factor 1.05–1.2), and clusters such as NGC 6266, NGC 6809, NGC 288, and NGC 7089 exhibit multi-sigma discrepancies between CMD-derived and parallax distances22.  
White-dwarf cooling sequences constrain age through degenerate electron conduction and core crystallization independently of main-sequence nuclear burning11. Deep HST observations of Messier 4 (M4) initially yielded 12.7 ± 0.7 Gyr (2-sigma random error) compared to 7.3 ± 1.5 Gyr for the Galactic disk23, refined via two-dimensional CMD modeling incorporating distance, extinction, mass, and envelope/core uncertainties to a best fit of 12.1 Gyr and a 95% lower limit of 10.3 Gyr12. Bedin et al. (2009) confirmed truncation consistent with M4's main-sequence turn-off age of 12.0 ± 1.4 Gyr24, while Richer et al. (2004) showed that an earlier \~9 Gyr estimate by De Marchi et al. (2003) stemmed from one-dimensional luminosity-function fitting25. In the metal-poor cluster NGC 6397, a 2016 MNRAS Monte Carlo fit to proper-motion-cleaned 126-orbit HST ACS photometry using Hansen et al. (2013) models yielded a best-fit cooling age of 12.93 Gyr11. Similarly, incorporating neon-22 sedimentation and carbon-oxygen phase separation in NGC 6791 resolved a longstanding discrepancy, shifting its white-dwarf cooling age to 8.0 ± 0.2 Gyr11, building on the foundational 9.5 Gyr lower limit from the Galactic disk white-dwarf luminosity function (Oswalt et al. 1996\)27.  
Radioactive actinide chronometry measures isotopic decay ratios directly without relying on stellar luminosity tracks. In the metal-poor halo red giant HE 1523−0901 (\[Fe/H\] \= −2.65 ± 0.22 dex; Gaia parallax 0.3278 ± 0.0197 mas, distance 3,100 ± 200 pc), Frebel et al. (2007) combined Very Large Telescope (VLT) measurements of U/Th, U/Ir, Th/Eu, and Th/Os ratios to infer a weighted age of 13.2 Gyr, carrying a systematic uncertainty range of 0.7 to 2.7 Gyr from theoretical initial r-process production yields13. The high-velocity subgiant HD 140283 yields a central age of 14.46 ± 0.31 Gyr (statistical) as reviewed in a 2020 preprint (arXiv:2002.10332)28, though subsequent stellar-evolution reviews emphasize substantial atmosphere and opacity systematics in subgiant modeling21.

### **Global Expansion Benchmarks, Cosmic Chronometers, and Distance Calibration**

Global age estimates from cosmic microwave background (CMB) and baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) datasets depend explicitly on an assumed metric cosmology. Planck 2018 CMB spectra within flat Lambda-CDM yield H0 \= 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc and an inferred age of 13.8 ± 0.02 Gyr1. Combining Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Data Release 2 (DR2) BAO measurements with Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) DR6 and Planck PR4 spectra produces a \~3-sigma preference for evolving dark energy under a Chevallier-Polarski-Linder (CPL) parameterization (rising above 4 sigma with Type Ia supernovae), though neutrino-mass bounds remain sensitive to multipole cuts (Garcia-Quintero et al. 2025\)31. Under CMB \+ DESI DR2 CPL fits, the inferred Hubble constant drops to 63.9 \+1.7/−2.1 km/s/Mpc, even though Bayesian evidence disfavors adding two free parameters in some likelihood comparisons15. Meanwhile, 32 differential-age H(z) cosmic chronometer measurements from passively evolving galaxies probe redshift-rate evolution without a supernova distance ladder, yet still require stellar-population synthesis priors and a cosmological integral to infer global t015.  
Local distance-ladder calibrations rest on redshift-free geometric anchors: 20 late-type detached eclipsing binaries (DEBs) fix the Large Magellanic Cloud (LMC) distance to 1% precision (inclination 25.0 ± 4 deg)34, while 22 GHz water megamaser VLBI kinematics in NGC 4258 yield a purely geometric angular-diameter distance of 7.576 ± 0.082 (stat.) ± 0.076 (sys.) Mpc (distance modulus 29.397 ± 0.032 mag)34. Calibrating Cepheids and Type Ia supernovae against these anchors, SH0ES reports H0 \= 74.03 ± 1.42 km/s/Mpc (2019) and 73.2 ± 0.9 km/s/Mpc (2024)30, and JWST observations of \>1,000 Cepheids in NGC 4258 and five supernova hosts rejected HST photometric crowding as the cause of the Hubble tension at 8-sigma confidence (Riess et al. 2024\)39. By contrast, the Carnegie-Chicago Hubble Program (CCHP) using Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) stars obtained H0 \= 69.8 ± 0.8 ± 1.7 km/s/Mpc in 201930 and 69.85 km/s/Mpc (TRGB) and 67.96 km/s/Mpc (JAGB) in 202438, while JWST TRGB calibration in NGC 4258 measured an extinction-corrected F090W tip of 25.035 mag (Anand et al. 2024\)40. Crucially, a 2025 preprint (arXiv:2510.04998) identified a \>0.1 mag discrepancy in NGC 4258 Wesenheit Cepheid zero-points across 2016–2022 SH0ES data and showed that uncertainties in the anomalous extinction ratio RV shift inferred distances by 4–5%38.

### **JWST High-Redshift Dropouts vs. Substellar Interlopers**

Broadband JWST/NIRCam surveys select high-redshift candidates via sharp short-wavelength flux dropouts, creating a spectral degeneracy at z \> 15–30 between Lyman-alpha breaks and deep methane/water absorption in ultra-cool Y-type brown dwarfs5. The F356W-dropout Capotauro (CEERS ID U-100588; Gandolfi et al. 2026a)16 and two Bullet Cluster F277W/F356W dropouts6 were initially modeled as z ≈ 30–32 galaxy candidates before 2026 multi-epoch NIRCam astrometry and NIRSpec spectroscopy reclassified all three as Milky Way Y dwarfs5. Conversely, multi-facility spectroscopy has confirmed luminous extended galaxies near z ≈ 14, notably JADES-GS-z14-08 and MoM-z1417, illustrating why proper-motion and line-confirmation audits are essential16.

### **Three-Cosmology Comparison on Chronometers and High-Redshift Scales**

Evaluating the three cosmological frameworks against verified chronometers and z ≈ 14 scales exposes sharp diagnostic trade-offs3. Under flat Lambda-CDM (Model 1), Planck 2018 parameters yield t0 \= 13.791 Gyr (chi-squared \= 3.73 across the 2025 globular-cluster bound, NGC 6397 white dwarfs, and HE 1523−0901)3, whereas SH0ES parameters (H0 \= 73.2 km/s/Mpc) yield t0 \= 12.698 ± 0.175 Gyr, elevating chi-squared to 10.36 and leaving the global model 0.87 Gyr younger than the 2025 globular-cluster bound (2.68-sigma tension)3. CCHP TRGB calibration (H0 \= 69.85 km/s/Mpc) yields an intermediate 13.307 Gyr3.  
Under a dynamical or changing-coupling hybrid (Model 2), a DESI-DR2-like lower H0 \= 63.9 km/s/Mpc with w \= −0.8 extends t0 to 14.064 Gyr3, while phantom dark energy (w \= −1.2) at H0 \= 73.2 km/s/Mpc lifts t0 only to 13.033 Gyr3. In changing-gravitational-coupling hybrids where effective gravitational strength varied in the past, stellar hydrogen-burning rates scale strongly with gravity, shifting inferred main-sequence ages relative to nuclear actinide clocks—a testable divergence between stellar luminosity tracks and weak/strong radioactive decay constants.  
Under the ArcSecs static-Euclidean decelerating-light proposal (Model 3), total cosmic duration Tcosmos is decoupled from HA, fitting all Milky Way chronometers with zero age tension3. For matched deceleration and attenuation rates (67.4 km/s/Mpc), the Euclidean light-travel distance to JADES-GS-z14-0 (z \= 14.1793) is 4,154.9 Mpc—only 40.2% of its flat Lambda-CDM comoving distance (10,344.1 Mpc) and 2.6% of its luminosity distance (156,928.2 Mpc)3. However, because flat Lambda-CDM's angular-diameter distance turns over above z ≈ 1.6 to dA \= 681.5 Mpc at z \= 14.1793, the pure Euclidean distance of 4,154.9 Mpc translates JADES-GS-z14-0's 0.079 arcsec half-light radius into 1,591.4 pc, which is 6.1 times larger than the 260 ± 20 pc inferred under standard dA (though 2.9 times smaller than the 4,633.6 pc implied by non-decelerating exponential tired light)3. Model 3 therefore achieves radial proximity relative to comoving and luminosity scales while still requiring a missing angular-magnification mechanism to satisfy Premise 11 relative to dA3.
