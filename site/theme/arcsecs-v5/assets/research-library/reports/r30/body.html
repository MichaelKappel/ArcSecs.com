2. Operational Definitions of Photon Mass and Discriminating Observables

In experimental physics, the label “mass” applied to photons encompasses three distinct operational properties that must remain conceptually and quantitatively separated.
The first operational definition is dynamical momentum-carrying mass-energy. Every detected photon transfers quantized energy proportional to its frequency and linear momentum equal to energy divided by the limiting electromagnetic speed in the massless limit, or governed more generally by the dispersion relation connecting squared energy to squared momentum and squared rest-mass energy6. Laboratory radiation pressure, Compton scattering, and photoelectric recoil directly verify that propagating light carries physical inertia and exchanges momentum with matter at every interaction. This operational property is empirically established and aligns with the ArcSecs premise that light acts as a momentum-bearing physical participant in nature.
The second operational definition is gravitational coupling mass-energy. Because photons carry energy flux and momentum density (parameterized mathematically via a stress-energy tensor), they respond to gravitational fields—exhibiting angular deflection near stars and galaxies, frequency shifts across gravitational potential differences, and excess propagation delays when passing near massive bodies such as the Sun1. Within an ArcSecs physical-first ontology lacking curved spacetime, this behavior arises from direct gravitational force or potential-gradient coupling to the photon’s physical energy-momentum. Crucially, however, gravitational coupling to photon energy-momentum operates identically at leading order whether the photon’s invariant rest mass is strictly zero or tiny compared to photon energy.
The third, narrowest operational definition is nonzero invariant rest mass. Formally defined as the residual energy a quantum would possess at zero linear momentum—or equivalently via the inverse reduced Compton wavelength governing finite-range interactions—invariant rest mass generates four distinct physical signatures absent in Maxwell electrodynamics11. Specifically, Proca-type massive vector dynamics breaks local gauge invariance (or requires a compensating Stueckelberg scalar field), introduces a third physical longitudinal polarization state, damps static electrostatic and magnetostatic potentials exponentially beyond the reduced Compton wavelength, and slows vacuum group velocity inversely with the square of photon frequency11. Discriminating zero from nonzero invariant rest mass therefore demands detecting an exponential static-field departure11, an uncancelled vector-potential torque or Aharonov-Bohm phase shift13, a vacuum frequency-squared arrival delay separable from cold-plasma dispersion17, or a physical longitudinal electromagnetic wave mode11.

3. Literature Review and Verified Experimental Constraint Register

Primary peer-reviewed literature, Particle Data Group (PDG) summaries, and astrophysical transient studies through November 2026 document progressive upper limits on invariant photon rest mass across four experimental regimes. Note on source provenance: the direct web endpoints for the 2025 PDG online listing19 and the LIGO Document Control Center entry P170030820 were inaccessible during verification; consequently, PDG benchmarks are verified from the accessible 2026 PDG Gauge and Higgs Bosons summary table and archived 2014–2024 PDG photon listings8, while GW170817 and GRB 170817A measurements are verified from published Astrophysical Journal Letters records2.

Laboratory Electrodynamics and Quantum Interferometry

Local terrestrial experiments test Maxwell’s inverse-square laws directly inside shielded enclosures. The cleanest local Cavendish-style electrostatic test remains Williams, Faller, and Hill (1971), which searched for interior electric fields inside concentric charged shells and constrained the photon Compton wavelength above roughly 20,000 kilometers (over 2 × 10⁷ meters in Goldhaber and Nieto’s review, cited as over 3 × 10⁹ centimeters in Spavieri et al.), corresponding to an upper rest-mass limit near 2 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kilograms, or roughly 10⁻¹⁴ electron-volts per speed-of-light squared5. Cryogenic tests of Ampère’s law at 1.24 kelvin by Chernikov et al. (1992)11 and low-temperature phase-transition measurements at 1.36 kelvin by Ryan, Accetta, and Austin (constraining the Compton wavelength above 300 meters) probed whether photon mass emerges below a critical temperature5. (Note: the exact numerical mass exponent from Chernikov et al. 1992 was truncated in the accessible abstract tables11 and is therefore not extrapolated here.)
Torsion-balance experiments introduced by Lakes (1998) and refined dynamically by Luo, Tu, Hu, and Luan (2003) measure mechanical torques on shielded magnetized toroids exposed to an assumed ambient cosmic magnetic vector potential, with Luo et al. reporting an inverse range exceeding 1.66 × 10¹³ centimeters and a photon mass below 2.1 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kilograms (2.1 × 10⁻⁵¹ grams)11. However, authoritative reviews by Goldhaber and Nieto (2010) and Tu, Luo, and Gillies (2005) classify extended Lakes-method bounds as speculative and strongly model-dependent because the ambient cosmic vector potential has never been independently measured and its local orientation is unknown5. Quantum-interferometric proposals using the Aharonov-Bohm effect—originally estimated by Boulware and Deser at a range near 140 kilometers (roughly 2.5 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kilograms) and over 10⁵ meters in Goldhaber and Nieto—have been extended theoretically by Spavieri et al. (2007, 2011) to projected tabletop sensitivities between 2 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kilograms and 10⁻⁵⁷ kilograms, though dedicated ultra-sensitive implementations remain prospective5.

Planetary, Solar-Wind, and Galactic Magnetohydrodynamics

Spacecraft magnetometry extends spatial baselines from meters to astronomical units. Applying Erwin Schrödinger’s vector-potential modification for a magnetic dipole, Goldhaber and Nieto (1968) analyzed geomagnetic data to bound photon mass below 4.0 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms (4.0 × 10⁻⁴⁸ grams)32, followed by Fischbach et al. (1994) on terrestrial magnetic fields and Davis, Goldhaber, and Nieto (1975) using Pioneer 10 observations of Jupiter’s magnetic field—widely cited as a secure planetary dipole limit, though exact mass exponents from those two tables were truncated in available summaries5.
The official PDG 2026 summary table adopts an upper bound of less than 1 × 10⁻¹⁸ electron-volts (roughly 1.78 × 10⁻⁵⁴ kilograms, corresponding to a reduced Compton wavelength exceeding 1.32 astronomical units)7. That benchmark derives from Ryutov (1997, 2007), who analyzed solar-wind magnetohydrodynamics out to Earth’s orbit and subsequently Pluto’s orbit, showing that a larger Proca mass would suppress large-scale interplanetary magnetic-field coherence9. Independent spacecraft tests include Cluster multi-point measurements of Ampère’s law in the solar wind by Retinò, Spallicci, and Vaivads (2016)21 and recent 2024 analyses using Parker Solar Probe observations where candidate photon-mass scales intersect local inner-heliosphere plasma frequencies34. Still tighter cosmic bounds—such as Goldhaber and Nieto (2003) at roughly 10⁻⁵⁵ kilograms (10⁻⁵² grams) from Coma Cluster plasma stability—carry substantial magnetohydrodynamic model assumptions5.

Fast Radio Bursts, Pulsars, and Gamma-Ray Bursts

Time-of-flight dispersion of short cosmological radio bursts avoids static-field equilibrium assumptions by probing vacuum wavepacket group velocity directly. Under Proca kinematics, lower-frequency photons travel slightly slower than higher-frequency photons, creating an arrival delay proportional to the inverse square of frequency—mathematically mirroring cold-plasma dispersion along the line of sight12.
Early gamma-ray burst (GRB) timing constraints by Schaefer (1999) and GRB multi-band radio afterglow analyses in 2016 (arXiv:1607.03225, reaching 2.804 × 10⁻⁴⁷ kilograms for GRB 000301C)12 were surpassed by fast radio bursts (FRBs), whose millisecond durations at gigahertz frequencies travel across gigaparsec distances15. Early single-burst limits using FRB 150418 (5.2 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kilograms, Wu et al. 2016, relying on a contested host redshift; 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁴ electron-volts in Bonetti et al. 2016) and FRB 121102 (3.9 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kilograms, Bonetti et al. 2017; 5.1 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms from subpulse time-frequency drift, Xing et al. 2019) suffered single-sightline degeneracy between plasma dispersion measure and photon rest mass15. Shao and Zhang (2017) resolved this degeneracy statistically by exploiting the distinct distance/redshift scaling of plasma versus Proca delays across burst ensembles, setting a 21-FRB bound of 8.7 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms at 68 percent confidence15.
Subsequent multi-source bounds included Magellanic Cloud radio pulsars (2.3 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kilograms in Wei and Wu 2018 preprint; 1.51 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kilograms cited in Lin et al. 2023)18, nine localized FRBs (7.1 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms or 4.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ electron-volts at 68 percent confidence, and 1.3 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kilograms at 95 percent confidence, Wei and Wu 2020)17, 129 predominantly unlocalized FRBs (3.1 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms at 68 percent confidence, Wang, Miao, and Shao 2021)14, and localized FRB updates reaching 3.8 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms at 1-sigma (7.2 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms at 2-sigma)40. Crucially, Zhang, Liu, Yu, and Wu (2025/2026) demonstrated that several prior FRB studies omitted a mandatory normalization factor in the Macquart extragalactic dispersion-measure distribution, biasing earlier limits artificially low14. Reanalyzing 104 localized FRBs with the corrected distribution alongside supernova, cosmic microwave background, and baryon acoustic oscillation benchmarks, Zhang et al. established robust 1-sigma upper bounds of 4.83 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms, 4.71 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms, and 4.86 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms (roughly 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ to 2.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ electron-volts per speed-of-light squared via CODATA conversion) across three background parameterizations6. Second-order FRB and Crab pulsar dispersion analyses (arXiv:2207.00950) additionally bound quartic dispersion terms at 6.0 × 10⁻⁴⁷ to 6.3 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kilograms15.

Gravitational Lensing and Multi-Messenger Timing

Solar-system Shapiro time-delay measurements via the Cassini spacecraft and Very Long Baseline Interferometry (VLBI) light-deflection campaigns confirm gravitational delay and deflection to within 0.001 percent (10⁻⁵ precision) achromatically across radio frequencies3. Massive-photon scattering calculations by Accioly and Paszko (2004, 2008) show that invariant rest mass adds an inverse-energy-squared correction to gravitational deflection41. In 2025, a Physical Review D study using arrival-time ratios of a strongly lensed FRB candidate tightened gravitational-lensing photon-mass bounds by one to two orders of magnitude compared with earlier lensed active galactic nucleus studies4. Meanwhile, the binary neutron star merger GW170817 and GRB 170817A (located roughly 40 megaparsecs away, 130 million light-years) recorded an arrival delay of plus 1.74 ± 0.05 seconds between gravitational waves and 100-kiloelectron-volt gamma rays (chance probability 5.0 × 10⁻⁸), bounding non-dispersive velocity offsets between negative 3 × 10⁻¹⁵ and positive 7 × 10⁻¹⁶ times the speed of light under source-lag assumptions2. Direct group-velocity conversion shows this 1.74-second lag at 100 kiloelectron-volts yields a naive Proca bound of 2.91 × 10⁻³ electron-volts (5.18 × 10⁻³⁹ kilograms)7, demonstrating that multi-messenger gamma-ray timing probes non-dispersive speed equality rather than Proca invariant rest mass.

Maxwell Calibration Control vs. Proca/Stueckelberg Candidate

Maxwell electrodynamics functions strictly as our calibrated laboratory baseline: it enforces local gauge symmetry, supports two transverse polarization states, produces infinite-range inverse-square static potentials, and propagates all vacuum frequencies at the limiting speed c. Introducing a Proca mass parameter directly breaks local gauge invariance, dynamically enforcing the Lorenz subsidiary condition on the vector potential and expanding the physical field to three degrees of freedom11. Sub-luminal dispersive propagation: Massive wavepackets travel below the limiting electromagnetic speed c, with fractional speed deficit proportional to half the square of the mass-to-energy ratio7. Gravitational Coupling Without Spacetime Curvature

Does any verified gravitational-lensing measurement detect a photon’s invariant rest mass? Quantitative evaluation shows the answer is no at present instrumental precision. In an ArcSecs physical-coupling description—operationally equivalent in weak fields to a spatially varying optical refractive gradient or direct radial gravitational force proportional to the Newtonian potential divided by c squared acting on photon energy-momentum—a passing photon exchanges transverse and longitudinal impulse with the central gravitational field. Incorporating nonzero invariant rest mass into weak-field scattering (following Accioly and Paszko 2004/2008) adds a chromatic enhancement to the deflection angle proportional to half the square of the rest-mass-to-energy ratio41. Plain-English ArcSecs Evidence Summary (Mission 01: Photon Mass and Gravity)

Does light possess physical mass, and does gravity pull on light directly without needing “curved spacetime”? This independent investigation audited laboratory electrodynamics, spacecraft magnetometry, gravitational lensing observations, and distant cosmic radio bursts to separate what measurements firmly establish from what remains untested.
What the evidence firmly supports: Photons—physical wavepackets of light—unambiguously carry energy and momentum, exert radiation pressure on matter, and respond directly to gravity1. When starlight or radio signals graze the Sun or pass distant galaxies, their paths bend and their arrival intervals delay relative to unobstructed routes3. Within the ArcSecs worldview, these are concrete physical interactions between massive gravitating bodies and light’s physical energy-momentum, occurring at ordinary spatial locations (“Where”) under constant event progression (“When”)—without treating space or time as stretchable fabrics.
What remains unmeasured: Physics distinguishes between light’s energy-momentum gravitational mass-equivalence (firmly observed) and a photon’s invariant rest mass—the residual mass a light packet would hold if brought to rest. Despite decades of searching, no experiment has positively detected nonzero invariant rest mass. Instead, measurements place strict upper ceilings on how large it could be: laboratory electric-field shields rule out masses above 2 × 10⁻⁵⁰ kilograms (roughly 10⁻¹⁴ electron-volts)5; 104 localized fast radio bursts tighten the ceiling to roughly 4.8 × 10⁻⁵¹ kilograms (2.7 × 10⁻¹⁵ electron-volts)6; and solar-wind magnetic coherence sets the Particle Data Group benchmark at 1 × 10⁻¹⁸ electron-volts8.
Two key boundaries identified by this study: First, adding a standard Proca rest mass causes lower-frequency radio waves to lag slightly behind higher-frequency light, but it does not make light lose energy or continually slow down over time in empty space. Consequently, Proca photon rest mass alone cannot explain cosmological redshifts; any ArcSecs redshift model requires a separate physical medium-coupling mechanism capable of passing galaxy surface-brightness dimming, supernova spectral-aging, and cosmic microwave background blackbody benchmarks45. Second, standard gravitational bending deflects optical and radio frequencies almost identically even with a tiny rest mass, meaning lensing angles confirm energy-momentum gravitational coupling rather than invariant rest mass3.
The constructive path forward: Rather than arguing philosophy, ArcSecs research can test for a genuine positive rest-mass signal by (1) tracking ultra-low-frequency repeating radio bursts below 50 megahertz to isolate coupled mass-plasma dispersion shifts15, and (2) operating cryogenic superconducting shielded cavities to search for the faint longitudinal wave pattern unique to massive light11.

  • Observed or calibrated quantities: Photon momentum, radiation pressure and gravitational deflection are observed; photon invariant rest mass is currently constrained by limits, not a positive measurement.

  • Reproducible comparison / sources: PDG photon listings; Maxwell/Proca laboratory null tests; localized FRB arrival-time constraints.
  • Falsification rule: Retain every published measured quantity and uncertainty. If a specified ArcSecs mechanism fails that benchmark, record the failing assumption and design a more complete physical mechanism or a discriminating observation; do not erase or recast the measurement as a successful fit.
  • Scientific release status: Research working edition. Source bibliography, numerical calculations, author attribution and any claimed original simulations require independent scientific verification before journal submission.
