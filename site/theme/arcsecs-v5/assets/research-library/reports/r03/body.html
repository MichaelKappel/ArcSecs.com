2. Physical argument and mechanism candidates

The strongest common-sense challenge to cosmic age and size is epistemological: an instrument measures received flux, angular position, frequencies, proper motions and some geometric parallaxes; it does not hold a ruler across the entire universe. The 13.8-billion-year estimate is not arbitrary, but it is derived inside a calibrated cosmological model and applies to its defined cosmic history. It does not by itself settle whether a greater parent cosmos existed, how many regions may exist outside our observable domain, or the total physical volume.

ArcSecs’ most concrete case concerns false high-redshift candidates. Capotauro was first proposed photometrically at roughly z~32; subsequent multi-epoch astrometry measured 132 ± 20 milliarcseconds of motion over 3.5 years, showing it is a Milky Way brown dwarf rather than a remote galaxy. A template-inferred distance near 730 pc is not itself a geometric parallax. This is powerful evidence that one must distinguish color selection, spectroscopy and geometric motion. It does not imply that all confirmed high-redshift galaxies are foreground objects. Such a distinction lets the ArcSecs case be sharpened without generalizing from a single misclassified object.

1. Introduction

Statements that the universe is 13.8 billion years old are among the most recognizable quantitative claims in modern physical science. Yet a rigorous epistemic audit reveals an essential distinction between what instruments directly record—photons arriving at measured sky coordinates, fluxes, passband colors, spectral features, trigonometric parallaxes, and angular proper motions—and what theoretical frameworks infer about cosmic duration. The ArcSecs research position evaluated in this study advances an explicit hypothesis: the universe’s precise age is not directly known independently of cosmological assumptions. Treating this proposition strictly as a testable hypothesis rather than an established conclusion requires separating three distinct questions that are frequently conflated in popular and technical discourse alike. First, what strict lower bounds on cosmic duration are enforced by local astrophysical clocks that do not rely on a cosmological expansion model? Second, how sensitive are global age inferences to the choice of expansion history, distance-ladder anchor, dark-energy dynamics, and redshift physics? Third, to what extent do photometric classification failures at the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observational frontier—exemplified by apparent ultra-high-redshift candidates later reclassified as foreground Milky Way substellar objects—alter or fail to alter constraints on cosmic chronology?
Answering these questions as of 22 November 2026 demands careful calibration against contradictory lines of evidence. Over the past three years, three major developments have reshaped cosmic chronology. First, refined cosmic microwave background (CMB) polarization and lensing measurements from the Atacama Cosmology Telescope Data Release 6 (ACT DR6) combined with Planck legacy maps and Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument Data Release 2 (DESI DR2) baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) have tightened expanding-model constraints while surfacing an approximately 3-sigma statistical preference (spanning 2.7 to 4.2 sigma across dataset combinations in collaboration analyses and secondary summaries) for evolving dark energy over a static cosmological constant19. Second, JWST observations of Cepheids, the Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB), and J-region Asymptotic Giant Branch (JAGB) carbon stars have narrowed distance-ladder discrepancies to the nearby Type Ia supernova calibration step, leaving local Hubble constant determinations split between 70.4 km/s/Mpc from the Chicago-Carnegie Hubble Program (CCHP) and 73.04 km/s/Mpc from the SH0ES Cepheid ladder, compared against 67.36 to 68.22 km/s/Mpc from early-universe CMB and BAO analyses2. Third, JWST follow-up spectroscopy and multi-epoch astrometry through late 2026 have simultaneously confirmed genuine luminous galaxies out to spectroscopic redshifts 14.44 (MoM-z14) and 14.1793 (JADES-GS-z14-0)11 while reclassifying several flagship photometric candidates above redshift 20—most notably Capotauro and two Bullet Cluster dropouts—as cold Milky Way Y-type brown dwarfs10.
This manuscript executes a structured, reproducible evaluation across five interlocking domains: operational definitions of cosmic age across finite-beginning, cyclic, and past-eternal frameworks; empirical lower-bound chronometers rooted in stellar and nuclear physics; the complete dependency architecture translating raw astrometric and photometric observables into cataloged distances; a primary-source forensic audit of JWST false high-redshift candidates versus spectroscopically confirmed galaxies; and a formal multi-cosmology model comparison utilizing correlated cosmic chronometers alongside held-out geometric and kinematic tests. All mathematical frameworks and derivations are presented in precise procedural prose without displaying equations directly in the report text, preserving full analytical rigor and dimensional transparency.

2. Operational Definitions and Conceptual Boundaries

A recurring source of confusion in cosmology is the failure to specify which physical clock defines the “age of the universe.” Operationally, empirical cosmology does not measure the absolute duration of all physical existence; it measures elapsed proper time along specific worldlines between defined physical transitions. Three nested timescales must therefore be distinguished rigorously.
First, the age of the observable hot phase denotes the elapsed proper time experienced by a comoving observer from the onset of a hot, dense, radiation-dominated state—characterized empirically by primordial light-element nucleosynthesis and the thermal cosmic microwave background—to the present epoch. In a standard spatially flat expanding Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) cosmology with a singular initial boundary, this hot-phase duration coincides with the formal parametric age of the spacetime metric. In non-singular bouncing or cyclic cosmologies, however, such as the ekpyrotic and slowly contracting cyclic frameworks constructed by Steinhardt and Turok and refined by Ijjas and Steinhardt (2019)20, the 13.8-billion-year interval represents solely the duration elapsed since the most recent bounce and reheating transition, not the total age of physical reality.
Second, the age of all physical reality encompasses any pre-hot-phase inflationary epoch, prior contraction-and-bounce cycles, or past-eternal static or emergent geometry. Theoretical bounds on past duration are kinematical or dynamical rather than directly observational. The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin (BGV) theorem (Borde, Guth, & Vilenkin 2003) establishes that any classical spacetime whose average expansion rate along past-directed null or timelike geodesics remains strictly positive must be past-geodesically incomplete, implying a boundary to the classical expanding patch25. Crucially, the BGV theorem does not specify what physical reality exists at or prior to that boundary, nor does it forbid geodesically complete cyclic models in which intervals of slow contraction violate the theorem’s average-expansion premise20. Consequently, whether physical reality had a finite beginning, underwent finite or infinite prior cycles, or is past-eternal remains empirically undecidable from current electromagnetic and gravitational-wave data.
Third, the ages of particular astrophysical systems—such as metal-poor globular clusters, halo subgiant stars, cooling white dwarfs, and actinide-bearing stellar atmospheres—measure elapsed time since local gravitational collapse, nuclear ignition, core degeneracy onset, or rapid neutron-capture (r-process) nucleosynthesis. Because physical objects contained within our observable domain cannot predate the physical phase that enabled their formation under standard causal thermodynamics and unchanged local nuclear decay physics, the oldest rigorously dated local objects establish a strict empirical minimum age for the universe, whereas no individual star or cluster can by itself establish an upper bound on cosmic duration3.
An equally vital conceptual boundary separates the light-year as a standardized unit of length from cosmological distance inference. By international astronomical definition, a light-year is simply a fixed spatial length—the distance light travels in vacuum during one Julian year (365.25 days) at the defined speed of light, approximately 9.461 trillion kilometers. Using light-years or megaparsecs as units of length carries no cosmological assumption. By contrast, inferring how many billions of light-years away a high-redshift galaxy lies—and whether that number represents light-travel distance (lookback time multiplied by the speed of light), present-day comoving proper distance, luminosity distance, or angular-diameter distance—depends entirely on the assumed spacetime metric and photon-propagation physics. For example, at the confirmed spectroscopic redshift 14.44 of MoM-z14, the reference flat expanding cosmology assigns a light-travel distance of 13.51 billion light-years, a present comoving distance of 33.84 billion light-years, a luminosity distance of 522.5 billion light-years, and an angular-diameter distance of 2.19 billion light-years, whereas a static Euclidean exponential tired-light model assigns a single geometric radial distance of 39.73 billion light-years and a luminosity distance of 156.11 billion light-years13. Conflating the unit of length with the model-dependent conversion from redshift to distance obscures where empirical measurement ends and cosmological modeling begins.

3. Literature Review

3.1 Global Cosmological Age Inferences and Emerging Tensions

The modern reference benchmark for the age of the observable hot phase derives from the Planck satellite’s 2018 final data release (PR3; Planck Collaboration 2020), which fitted six-parameter spatially flat ΛCDM to temperature, polarization, and lensing power spectra to obtain a Hubble constant of 67.36 ± 0.54 km/s/Mpc (often rounded to 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc), a total matter density fraction of 0.3153 ± 0.0073, and an inferred cosmic age of 13.80 ± 0.02 Gyr1. Subsequent independent high-multipole CMB measurements from ACT DR6 (Calabrese et al. 2025; five years of 2017–2022 observations across three frequency bands spanning 77 to 277 GHz, including a 43-sigma CMB lensing detection) combined with Planck PR4 and BAO data from DESI yielded an even tighter flat ΛCDM Hubble constant of 68.22 ± 0.36 km/s/Mpc with no statistically favored departure from baseline ΛCDM across the CMB-anchored extensions tested by the ACT team15.
However, combining DESI DR2 three-year BAO measurements (DESI Collaboration 2025, released 19 March 2025) and DESI DR2 Lyman-alpha forest Alcock–Paczynski geometric measurements at effective redshift 2.33 (DESI Collaboration 2026, released 30 July 2026) with Planck, ACT, SPT-3G, and Type Ia supernovae has shifted the dark-energy debate. Peer-reviewed and collaboration baseline analyses combining DESI DR2 BAO with Planck PR4 and ACT find an approximately 3-sigma preference for evolving dark energy over a static cosmological constant23, while secondary summaries of the broader DESI DR2 dataset (14 million galaxies and quasars plus supernovae) report preferences ranging from 2.7 to 4.2 sigma19. Simultaneously, local distance-ladder calibrations exhibit unresolved divergence: the SH0ES team’s Cepheid-calibrated Type Ia supernova ladder yields 73.04 ± 1.04 km/s/Mpc (with JWST confirming HST Cepheid photometry at the 0.02-magnitude, or 1 percent distance, level; Riess et al. 2022, 2024)13, whereas the blinded CCHP JWST plus HST TRGB calibration across 24 calibrating supernovae yields 70.4 km/s/Mpc with a conservative uncertainty below 3 percent, attributing residual discrepancies primarily to nearby supernova apparent magnitude systematics—where a 0.03-magnitude shift equals the entire 1.4 percent SH0ES uncertainty—rather than primary stellar distances (Freedman et al. 2025)14. Early Dark Energy (EDE) models attempt to reconcile CMB and SH0ES measurements by shrinking the pre-recombination sound horizon (Poulin et al. 2025)38, though ACT DR6 evaluations report that earlier hints of EDE in ACT DR4 polarization spectra are not favored by higher-precision DR6 data14.

3.2 Model-Independent and Partially Independent Astrophysical Chronometers

Independent astrophysical clocks provide essential reality checks on cosmological age inferences without relying on global expansion histories. Metal-poor Milky Way globular clusters represent the most precise stellar chronometers. Valcin et al. (2021) applied hierarchical Bayesian full-color-magnitude-diagram (CMD) modeling calibrated against Gaia Data Release 3 (DR3) parallaxes to find an oldest globular cluster age of 13.39 ± 0.10 (statistical) ± 0.23 (systematic) Gyr, corresponding to an inferred cosmic age of 13.50 (+0.16/−0.14 statistical, ±0.23 to 0.33 systematic) Gyr once cluster formation delay is included1. A March 2026 preprint follow-up (arXiv:2603.04872) reported that accounting for multiple stellar populations inside globular clusters has a negligible effect on these oldest-age determinations41. Independently, Ying et al. (2023) performed 20,000 Monte Carlo Dartmouth Stellar Evolution Program isochrone simulations for the benchmark metal-poor cluster M92 (NGC 6341), obtaining an absolute age of 13.80 ± 0.75 Gyr (5.4 percent total uncertainty) and demonstrating that distance uncertainty accounts for approximately 80 percent of the total error budget, with total metallicity, alpha-element abundance, and helium diffusion comprising the remaining major physical contributors3. Subsequent analysis of eight Milky Way globular clusters spanning metallicities from −2.31 to −0.77 dex (Ying et al. 2025) confirmed that distance and reddening drive over 50 percent of age uncertainty in every cluster43.
Field stars, white dwarfs, and radioactive isotopes provide independent cross-checks with distinct systematic profiles. The metal-poor halo subgiant HD 140283 (roughly 200 light-years distant), once cited as an apparent age paradox at 14.5 ± 0.8 Gyr, was revised downward to 12.0 ± 0.5 Gyr using MESA models by Tang and Joyce (2021, as summarized in subsequent cluster-age reviews) and to 12.3 Gyr using tailored elemental abundances rather than solar-scaled mixtures (which artificially inflated its model age to 14 Gyr)4. White-dwarf cooling sequences offer an independent degenerate-matter thermodynamic clock: deep Hubble Space Telescope imaging of NGC 6397 yielded a cooling age of 12.7 ± 0.7 Gyr (Hansen et al. 2005, 2007, 2013)4, while field white dwarf luminosity functions analyzed with Gaia astrometry yield 12.5 (+1.4/−3.4) Gyr for the Galactic inner halo alongside 6.8 to 8.2 Gyr for the thin disk and 8.7 to 9.9 Gyr for the thick disk (Kilic et al. 2017, 2019)49, extending Oswalt et al.’s (1996) classic 9.5 Gyr Galactic disk lower limit5. Critically, atmospheric composition systematics remain non-trivial: earlier theoretical modeling showed that if the halo consisted purely of helium-atmosphere white dwarfs, the lower age limit would relax to 7.5 Gyr51. Long-lived actinide radiochronometry bypasses stellar evolutionary tracks altogether by comparing observed ratios of uranium-238 (half-life 4.468 Gyr) and thorium-232 (half-life 14.05 Gyr) against stable r-process elements; Frebel et al. (2007) derived a multi-pair age of approximately 13.2 ± 2.0 Gyr for HE 1523-09016, while Cowan et al. obtained 13.84 ± 4 Gyr for BD +17°32484.
At cosmological redshifts, the differential cosmic chronometer (CC) technique developed extensively by Moresco, Borghi, Jiao, and Loubser measures the Hubble expansion rate directly from the relative age evolution of massive, passively evolving galaxies across adjacent redshift bins without invoking luminosity distances or an assumed dark-energy model7. Through 2026, this compilation spans 32+ measurements up to redshift 1.965 (Moresco et al. 2022)55, including LEGA-C Lick-index and full-spectrum determinations at redshift 0.75 yielding 98.8 ± 33.6 km/s/Mpc (Borghi, Moresco, & Cimatti 2022; Jiao et al. 2023)8 and three correlated DESI measurements from 360,000 massive passive galaxies at redshifts 0.46, 0.67, and 0.83 with total systematic uncertainty budgets of 13.9 percent (Loubser et al. 2025), while recent variable-abundance analyses show that propagating observed alpha-element enhancement raises stellar-population systematic uncertainty from roughly 8.7 percent to roughly 13 percent (arXiv:2608.10163)7.

3.3 Alternative Cosmologies and Direct Expansion Tests

Unexpectedly luminous early galaxies discovered by JWST above redshift 10 prompted renewed interest in alternative cosmologies that stretch cosmic age—most prominently Gupta’s (2023) covarying-coupling-constants plus tired-light hybrid model (CCC+TL), which proposes a 26.7 Gyr universe with 5.8 Gyr already elapsed by redshift 10 and 3.5 Gyr by redshift 204. However, independent physical tests severely constrain both pure static tired-light and hybrid models. Lubin and Sandage (2001) used HST observations of 34 early-type galaxies across three high-redshift clusters (redshifts 0.76, 0.90, and 0.92) to exclude pure tired light at greater than 10 sigma via the Tolman surface-brightness dimming test59. White et al. (2024) measured cosmological time dilation across 1,504 Dark Energy Survey (DES-SN5YR) Type Ia supernovae, finding light-curve width scaling with one plus redshift raised to the power 1.003 ± 0.01517, corroborated independently in high-redshift quasars by Lewis and Brewer (2023)61. Furthermore, Lei et al. (2026) demonstrated that CCC+TL parameters calibrated to Type Ia supernovae suffer a severe chi-squared failure (76.02 versus 14.50 for ΛCDM) when confronted with cosmic chronometer expansion-rate measurements58.

3.4 JWST High-Redshift Confirmations and Brown Dwarf Interlopers

JWST’s near-infrared sensitivity opened photometric Lyman-break searches to redshifts 15 through 30, simultaneously revealing genuine high-redshift galaxies and severe low-redshift or Galactic contamination channels. Early warning came from CEERS-93316, initially flagged as a redshift 16.4 candidate before NIRSpec spectroscopy confirmed it to be a dusty, strong-emission-line galaxy at spectroscopic redshift 4.9 (Arrabal Haro et al. 2023)64. Subsequent deep surveys confirmed authentic high-redshift galaxies through multi-line spectroscopy and independent submillimeter detections—anchored by JADES-GS-z14-0 at spectroscopic redshift 14.1793 ± 0.0007 (combining 28 hours of JWST NIRSpec spectroscopy with a 6.6-sigma ALMA OIIIO III 88 µm detection; Carniani et al. 2024; Schouws et al. 2025)16 and MoM-z14 at spectroscopic redshift 14.44 ± 0.02 (confirmed by a sharp Lyman-alpha break and five rest-ultraviolet emission lines; Naidu et al. 2026)11. By contrast, systematic scrutiny of extreme dropout candidates above redshift 15 across ~0.2 square degrees of ASTRODEEP-JWST fields by Castellano et al. (2025) found zero clean candidates above redshift 20, identified a NIRCam chip-gap transient (A2744_27713) alongside CEERS-93316, and confirmed via NIRSpec PRISM follow-up that five F200W dropout candidates were lower-redshift interlopers (including four dusty star-forming galaxies)68. In 2026, two decisive follow-up campaigns showed that cold Milky Way Y-type brown dwarfs with deep methane and water absorption troughs can mimic extreme redshift 20 to 32 dropouts: Bradač et al. (2026) reclassified two Bullet Cluster candidates (Bullet-BD1 and Bullet-BD2) using NIRSpec spectra and 1-year NIRCam proper motions10, and Liu et al. (2026) used a 3.5-year NIRCam baseline to measure greater than 6-sigma proper motion for the claimed redshift 32 candidate Capotauro12.

4. Explicit Hypotheses and Preregistered Analytical Protocol

To prevent post-hoc narrative fitting and adhere to strict statistical reproducibility standards, we declare our hypotheses, primary outcomes, exclusion rules, and decision thresholds in advance of model evaluation.

  1. Primary ArcSecs Hypothesis (H1 — Model Dependence of Global Age): The inferred global age of the universe varies by more than 0.5 Gyr across empirically viable cosmological models and distance-ladder calibrations, and the total duration of physical reality prior to the observable hot phase is not uniquely determined by current astronomical observations.
  2. Null Hypothesis for Global Age (H0 — Model-Independent Precision): Independent astrophysical chronometers and geometric probes converge on a single, model-independent cosmic age to within ±0.2 Gyr without requiring assumptions regarding spacetime expansion history, dark-energy evolution, or early-universe initial conditions.
  3. Sub-Hypothesis on JWST Ultra-High-Redshift Contamination (H2): Extreme photometric dropout candidates initially assigned redshifts above 20 exhibit an interloper contamination fraction significantly exceeding that of spectroscopically vetted redshift 10 to 14 samples, driven by cold Milky Way brown dwarfs and dusty strong-line galaxies, yet this contamination does not invalidate multi-line spectroscopic confirmations below redshift 15.
  4. Sub-Hypothesis on Alternative Redshift Mechanisms (H3): Non-expanding (static tired-light) and strongly hybrid (tired-light fraction exceeding 10 percent of total redshift) models fail out-of-sample predictive checks on cosmological time dilation, Alcock–Paczynski geometry, and differential cosmic chronometers, disqualifying them as viable avenues for extending cosmic age beyond 20 Gyr.

Decision Thresholds and Controls: Model preference is evaluated via likelihood-based chi-squared goodness of fit, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and approximate log Bayes factors, adopting a threshold of Delta BIC greater than 10 (approximate log Bayes factor worse than minus 5) for strong model exclusion, and 5-sigma discrepancy on held-out kinematic or geometric observables as an experimental falsification trigger. 5.1 Model-Dependent Expanding-Universe Cosmic Age Integration

Within any homogeneous, isotropic FLRW spacetime, cosmic time is linked to observed spectroscopic redshift through the inverse scale factor. Deriving the age of the observable hot phase proceeds in four explicit steps. First, one converts the present-day Hubble constant from observational units of kilometers per second per megaparsec into inverse gigayears by dividing the duration of one gigayear (3.15576 × 10^16 seconds) by the length of one megaparsec (3.0856776 × 10^19 kilometers); taking the reciprocal yields the fundamental Hubble-time scaling constant of 977.792222 Gyr per (km/s/Mpc)13. Second, one specifies the dimensionless expansion-rate function across cosmic history by summing four normalized energy-density terms—relativistic radiation scaling with the fourth power of one plus redshift (fixed at a present-day density fraction of 9.24 × 10^-5 for standard photons and three massless neutrino species), non-relativistic matter scaling with the third power of one plus redshift, spatial curvature scaling with the second power of one plus redshift, and dark energy whose density evolves according to a two-parameter equation-of-state trajectory characterized by its present value and linear scale-factor derivative—and then taking the positive square root13. Third, one evaluates the definite integral from redshift zero (today) to infinity (the formal initial singularity of the hot phase) of the reciprocal product of one plus redshift and the dimensionless expansion-rate function. Fourth, multiplying this dimensionless integral by the Hubble time produces the inferred cosmic age in gigayears.
Crucially, this derivation demonstrates why cosmic age is a model-dependent inference rather than a direct stopwatch measurement: the integrand must be extrapolated over an infinite redshift domain where only discrete windows (local supernovae, intermediate-redshift BAO and chronometers, and the redshift ~1,100 recombination surface) are empirically sampled. Evaluating finite upper limits produces the lookback time to any specific redshift, while integrating from a galaxy’s redshift to infinity yields the cosmic age at emission. Similarly, integrating the reciprocal of the dimensionless expansion rate alone (without the extra redshift factor) from zero to a target redshift and multiplying by the Hubble distance (numerically identical in giga-light-years to the Hubble time in gigayears because the speed of light is exactly one light-year per year) yields the comoving radial distance, from which luminosity distance follows by multiplying by one plus redshift and angular-diameter distance follows by dividing by one plus redshift13.

5.2 Stellar Evolution and Radiochronometry Age-Inference Framework

Astrophysical lower-bound chronometers bypass global expansion integrals entirely, replacing cosmological priors with stellar-interior or nuclear-decay physics. In globular cluster main-sequence turnoff (MSTO) dating, stars born nearly simultaneously exhaust core hydrogen at a rate governed tightly by initial stellar mass. Because the bolometric luminosity of the turnoff point declines monotonically with age, translating observed apparent magnitudes and colors into absolute age requires six coupled physical inputs: geometric distance modulus (mapping apparent flux to intrinsic luminosity), foreground dust reddening and extinction law, bulk iron-to-hydrogen metallicity, alpha-element enhancement (especially oxygen, which regulates carbon–nitrogen–oxygen cycle efficiency and envelope opacity), primordial helium mass fraction (higher helium lowers hydrogen fuel supply and raises mean molecular weight, accelerating evolution), and microscopic interior physics including atomic helium diffusion, convective mixing-length calibration, and nuclear fusion cross-sections (notably proton capture on nitrogen-14)3. Standard main-sequence turnoff scaling dictates that a 0.05-magnitude shift in distance modulus shifts derived cluster age by roughly 0.63 Gyr, a 0.10-dex shift in alpha-element abundance shifts age by roughly 0.45 Gyr, a 0.10-dex shift in iron metallicity shifts age by roughly 0.35 Gyr, a 0.015-magnitude shift in reddening shifts age by roughly 0.28 Gyr, and a 0.01 increase in primordial helium lowers derived age by roughly 0.12 Gyr13.
In white-dwarf cooling chronometry, degenerate carbon–oxygen stellar remnants lacking sustained core nuclear fusion radiate stored thermal energy into space. Total stellar age equals the sum of the progenitor’s main-sequence lifetime and the white dwarf’s cooling duration, determined from the faint termination cutoff of the cluster or halo luminosity function. Systematic uncertainties enter through white-dwarf atmospheric composition (hydrogen-rich versus helium-rich envelopes, which alter radiative opacity dramatically), core crystallization latent heat release, chemical phase separation in the interior, and rapid late-time Debye cooling that pushes the oldest massive remnants below observational detection limits5.
In long-lived actinide radiochronometry, radioactive decay provides a laboratory-calibrated clock independent of stellar interior convection or geometric distance errors. Comparing an observed stellar atmospheric abundance ratio of uranium-238 (half-life 4.468 Gyr) to thorium-232 (half-life 14.05 Gyr) against the theoretical initial r-process production ratio yields the elapsed time since nucleosynthesis by multiplying the difference in base-ten logarithmic abundance ratios by a fixed nuclear decay conversion factor of 21.763 Gyr per dex13. Because this multiplier is steep, even a modest 0.05-dex spectroscopic abundance uncertainty propagates to ±1.09 Gyr in age, a 0.10-dex error yields ±2.18 Gyr, and a 20 percent theoretical uncertainty in the initial r-process uranium-to-thorium production ratio contributes ±1.72 Gyr13. Longer-lived rhenium-187 (half-life 41.6 Gyr) decaying to osmium-187 suffers even steeper sensitivity to initial galactic chemical evolution history.

5.3 Alternative Cosmological Distance–Redshift and Clock-Rate Formulations

To test cosmic age across genuinely distinct physical mechanisms without borrowing unverified distance laws from external projects, we formulate two explicit non-standard alternatives alongside flat ΛCDM and evolving dark energy:

  1. Static Euclidean Exponential Tired-Light (Static-TL): Photon energy decreases continuously per unit path length through a hypothetical distance-proportional dissipation mechanism characterized by an attenuation constant matching the local Hubble slope, inside a non-expanding Euclidean space. Integrating the fractional wavelength shift along the radial path yields a logarithmic distance–redshift relation: physical radial distance equals the characteristic attenuation length multiplied by the natural logarithm of one plus redshift13. Because space does not expand, photon arrival rates are unchanged, angular sizes decrease inversely with radial distance without geometric focusing, and apparent bolometric flux dims only by the inverse square of radial distance times a single factor of one plus redshift from individual photon energy loss; consequently, luminosity distance equals radial distance times the square root of one plus redshift13. Crucially, this framework makes three rigid, parameter-free predictions: cosmological time dilation is strictly zero (light-curve stretch exponent zero), the Tolman surface-brightness dimming exponent is 1.0 rather than 4.059, and the Alcock–Paczynski geometric ratio (transverse distance divided by radial distance per unit redshift) equals one plus redshift multiplied by the natural logarithm of one plus redshift—yielding 4.0059 at redshift 2.33, completely independent of the attenuation constant13. Moreover, a strictly static universe with no universal expansion epoch predicts zero systematic differential age–redshift slope for passive galaxies (no cosmological expansion clock).
  2. Hybrid Changing-Coupling / Expansion + Tired-Light (CCC+TL): Following Gupta (2023) and evaluated by Lei et al. (2026)4, total observed redshift factorizes into an expanding metric component and a non-expanding tired-light component, accompanied by hypothesized secular variation in fundamental coupling constants that slows early expansion and extends total cosmic age to approximately 26.7 Gyr. Because only the metric expansion component stretches photon arrival intervals between successive wavefronts, any hybrid model in which tired light supplies a fraction between 10 percent and 30 percent of the logarithmic redshift factor directly predicts a suppressed supernova time-dilation exponent between 0.90 and 0.70 rather than unity13.

6.2 Statistical Inference, Covariance Propagation, and Mock Validation

Parameter uncertainties on expanding-universe cosmic ages are propagated via 2,000-draw bivariate Monte Carlo sampling incorporating the strong anti-correlation (correlation coefficient −0.95) between the Hubble constant and matter density inherent in acoustic-peak CMB constraints, while treating local distance-ladder Hubble constant estimates and CMB matter density as uncorrelated when evaluating hybrid local-plus-CMB priors13. Strict empirical lower bounds for local chronometers are computed as one-sided 95 percent and 99 percent Gaussian lower confidence bounds from combined quadrature statistical and systematic uncertainties.
For JWST ultra-high-redshift interloper contamination rates, small-sample binomial statistics require avoiding naive asymptotic normal intervals, which collapse unphysically at 100 percent observed failure rates. We therefore report both Bayesian posterior means and 68/95 percent credible intervals under a non-informative Jeffreys Beta(0.5, 0.5) prior alongside exact frequentist Clopper–Pearson 95 percent confidence intervals13.
To validate unbiased cosmic-age recovery under realistic correlated observational errors, we execute a 1,000-realization mock-injection and recovery test13. Synthetic datasets mimicking the correlated Loubser et al. (2025) DESI cosmic chronometer covariance matrix, the independent Borghi et al. (2022) LEGA-C measurement, and the held-out DESI DR2 Lyman-alpha Alcock–Paczynski geometric ratio are generated from an injected Planck ΛCDM ground truth (Hubble constant 67.36 km/s/Mpc, matter density 0.3153, true cosmic age 13.795 Gyr) and fitted via Nelder–Mead maximum-likelihood optimization. Across 1,000 realizations, the pipeline recovers a median Hubble constant of 66.98 (+8.41/−8.36) km/s/Mpc, a matter density of 0.315 ± 0.020, and a median cosmic age of 13.89 (+1.98/−1.56) Gyr—exhibiting a minor recovery bias of +0.096 Gyr and an empirical 1-sigma coverage of 70.3 percent, confirming well-calibrated inference without hidden optimizer bias13.

7.1 Expanding-Universe Cosmic Ages Versus Independent Astrophysical Lower Bounds

Evaluating the expanding-universe age integral across current observational calibrations shows that global age estimates remain tightly coupled to cosmological parameter priors and local distance-ladder anchors, whereas stellar and nuclear chronometers establish a robust minimum-age floor independent of expansion dynamics. For the ACT DR6 + DESI DR2 scenario, pairing the verified Calabrese et al. (2025) Hubble constant of 68.22 ± 0.36 km/s/Mpc15 with a matching flat-ΛCDM matter density of 0.3050 ± 0.0055 yields 13.749 ± 0.022 Gyr13.

Two striking scientific conclusions emerge directly from this synthesis. First, no viable cosmology can accommodate an age below roughly 12.6 Gyr without colliding with independent stellar clocks. The tightest stellar constraint—Valcin et al.’s (2021) hierarchical Bayesian analysis of the oldest Milky Way globular clusters anchored to Gaia DR3 distances—gives a cluster age of 13.39 ± 0.25 Gyr (combined statistical and systematic error) before even adding the pre-cluster formation delay, imposing a strict one-sided 95 percent confidence lower bound of 12.98 Gyr (12.81 Gyr at 99 percent confidence)1. Adopting the more conservative full-Monte-Carlo stellar-physics error budget of Ying et al. (2023) for M92 (13.80 ± 0.75 Gyr) yields a 95 percent lower bound of 12.57 Gyr (12.06 Gyr at 99 percent confidence)3. Independent white-dwarf cooling in NGC 6397 (12.70 ± 0.70 Gyr; 95 percent lower bound 11.55 Gyr)4 and actinide radiochronometry in HE 1523-0901 (13.2 ± 2.0 Gyr; 95 percent lower bound 9.91 Gyr)6 reinforce this floor through entirely different physical mechanisms—degenerate electron cooling and weak-interaction alpha decay—excluding a matter-dominated Einstein–de Sitter universe (9.68 ± 0.08 Gyr) by more than 4 Gyr.
Second, the ongoing Hubble tension creates an active 1.07-Gyr spread inside flat expanding ΛCDM itself. Because cosmic age decreases by 0.205 Gyr for every +1 km/s/Mpc increase in the Hubble constant at fixed matter density, combining the SH0ES local Cepheid calibration (73.04 ± 1.04 km/s/Mpc) with the Planck matter density fraction (0.3153) pulls the inferred flat ΛCDM age down to 12.722 ± 0.106 Gyr—placing it directly in tension with the 12.98 Gyr Valcin et al. 95 percent globular-cluster floor and leaving virtually zero formation margin for M9213. Reconciling a 73 km/s/Mpc expansion rate with globular cluster ages requires either lowering the matter density toward 0.28 (raising the age to 13.151 ± 0.271 Gyr, since each 0.01 drop in matter density adds 0.123 Gyr), invoking early-universe modifications such as Early Dark Energy that alter the pre-recombination sound horizon, or accepting a lower local expansion rate closer to the CCHP TRGB value of 70.4 km/s/Mpc (which yields 13.199 ± 0.360 Gyr, comfortably compatible with stellar clocks)13. Notably, switching from constant dark energy to an illustrative DESI DR2 + CMB evolving dark energy parameterization shifts the inferred age only modestly to 13.657 ± 0.172 Gyr because the opposing effects of a less negative present equation-of-state parameter (minus 0.203 Gyr per +0.1 step) and a negative evolutionary derivative partially cancel over the full redshift integral13.

Dissecting the Stellar Chronometer Error Budget

A critical insight from Ying et al. (2023) and subsequent multi-cluster analyses is that stellar chronometers are not yet limited primarily by exotic nuclear physics3. For M92, decomposing the 0.75-Gyr total uncertainty shows that geometric distance error accounts for 80 percent of the variance (±0.671 Gyr), whereas all stellar physics and chemical composition inputs combined contribute only 20 percent of the variance (±0.335 Gyr)3. An illustrative single-cluster sensitivity budget combining distance modulus (±0.05 mag contributing ±0.63 Gyr), alpha-element enhancement (±0.10 dex contributing ±0.45 Gyr), iron metallicity (±0.10 dex contributing ±0.35 Gyr), helium diffusion (±0.30 Gyr), foreground reddening (±0.015 mag contributing ±0.28 Gyr), convective mixing length and nuclear reaction rates (±0.25 Gyr), and primordial helium mass fraction (±0.01 contributing ±0.12 Gyr) sums in quadrature to ±0.98 Gyr—demonstrating why Gaia DR4 parallax improvements and precise reddening maps represent the single highest-leverage pathway to tightening cosmological-model-independent minimum ages13.

7.3 Case Study 1: Forensic Audit of Capotauro (Photometric Redshift 32 to Milky Way Y Dwarf)

The compact red source Capotauro, initially discovered in deep JWST Cosmic Evolution Early Release Science (CEERS) imaging by Gandolfi et al. (2025/2026, arXiv:2509.01664), became an immediate stress test for early-galaxy formation models because its abrupt flux drop shortward of 3.5 µm (an extreme F356W dropout) mimicked a Lyman-alpha break at photometric redshift ~32—corresponding in flat ΛCDM to just 89.8 million years after the Big Bang, with a comoving distance of 37.98 billion light-years13. Early astrometric upper limits (below 137 mas/yr) were too loose to rule out a foreground substellar object94.
Strong astrometric resolution arrived via peer-reviewed multi-epoch JWST NIRCam measurements published by Liu et al. (2026, MNRAS, Vol. 553, Issue 3, stag2055; accepted 14 October 2026, published online 28 October 2026)12. Comparing Epoch 1 discovery imaging from CEERS (JWST ERS Programme ID 1345, December 2022, filters F115W through F444W) and Cycle 1 Programme GO 2234 (June 2023, deep F090W) against Epoch 2 Cycle 4 imaging from MINERVA (GO 7814) and SPAM (GO 8559) taken in mid-to-late June 2026 established an exact 3.5-year epoch baseline12. Using filter-dependent point-spread-function (PSF) fitting registered to a local relative astrometric frame anchored by 68 compact reference sources, validated via a joint four-image injection–recovery analysis, Liu et al. detected an angular displacement of 132 ± 20 mas (reported as 131.7 mas in secondary summaries) over 3.5 years12.
This measurement yields proper-motion components of +9.4 (+6.4/−4.8) mas/yr and −35.6 (+5.1/−5.9) mas/yr, for a total proper motion of 37.6 (+5.5/−5.6) mas/yr at greater than 6-sigma significance, excluding an extragalactic origin (whether high-redshift galaxy, active galactic nucleus, or supernova)12. Combining this Galactic motion with Capotauro’s broadband spectral energy distribution classifies it as a cold Milky Way Y-type brown dwarf of photometric spectral type Y 1.0 ± 0.5 and effective temperature ~350 K12. Two strict epistemic caveats must be emphasized to prevent popular misreporting: first, Capotauro is a substellar brown dwarf, not a confirmed exoplanet or rogue planet12; second, its estimated distance of 730 ± 110 pc (~2,380 light-years) was derived spectrophotometrically by matching its SED to empirical brown-dwarf templates, not by direct trigonometric parallax, which Liu et al. explicitly note will require future multi-epoch observations12.

7.5 Contamination Fraction Estimates With Honest Sampling Uncertainty

Quantifying how often extreme photometric dropout selections fail requires accounting rigorously for small sample sizes. Across documented JWST follow-up cohorts through November 2026, every photometric candidate above redshift 20 with completed astrometric or spectroscopic follow-up has been reclassified away from ultra-high redshift—yet small denominators leave wide confidence bounds that must be reported honestly rather than overstated13. Specifically, the two Bullet Cluster dropouts (2/2) yield a Bayesian Jeffreys posterior mean of 83.3 percent (95 percent credible interval 33.3 to 100.0 percent; exact Clopper–Pearson 95 percent interval 15.8 to 100.0 percent); pooling Capotauro with the Bullet Cluster pair yields 3/3 followed-up candidates above redshift 20 (Jeffreys mean 87.5 percent; 95 percent credible interval 46.4 to 100.0 percent; Clopper–Pearson 29.2 to 100.0 percent); and pooling all eight followed-up extreme dropouts above redshift 15 (three substellar brown dwarfs plus the five NIRSpec-reclassified F200W dropouts in Castellano et al. 2025) yields 8/8 (Jeffreys mean 94.4 percent; 95 percent credible interval 73.8 to 100.0 percent; Clopper–Pearson 63.1 to 100.0 percent)10.

Two analytical caveats prevent misinterpreting these numbers. First, what high contamination above redshift 20 implies: photometric dropout selection beyond redshift 15 operates in a regime where true galaxy number densities plummet while foreground Y-dwarf and dusty low-redshift interloper populations dominate point-source counts, rendering unconfirmed photometric candidates above redshift 20 unsuitable as evidence against standard cosmic timelines. Second, what it does not imply: spectroscopically confirmed galaxies below redshift 15 (such as MoM-z14 and JADES-GS-z14-0) are verified via multiple rest-frame emission lines, submillimeter fine-structure lines, and spatially resolved, elongated morphologies that brown dwarfs cannot mimic11. Their unexpectedly high luminosity density (more than 100 times pre-JWST consensus predictions at redshifts 14 to 15; specifically 182 +329/−105 times in Naidu et al. 202611) genuinely challenges astrophysical models of early star-formation efficiency, burstiness, and initial mass functions, but does not by itself falsify the 280-to-290-Myr ΛCDM cosmic epoch—nor do brown-dwarf interlopers above redshift 20 erase that astrophysical puzzle.

9. Discussion, Epistemic Classification, and Falsification Criteria

9.1 Epistemic Audit of Core Findings

Evaluating the ArcSecs assertion strictly through evidence reveals a nuanced, bifurcated verdict rather than a binary triumph for either naive textbook certainty or radical skepticism.

  1. Direct Observations: Raw astrometric shifts of 132 ± 20 mas over 3.5 years for Capotauro12, 49 ± 8 and 24 ± 3 mas/yr for Bullet-BD1 and Bullet-BD210, Type Ia supernova light-curve stretching scaling as one plus redshift raised to 1.003 ± 0.01517, spectroscopic line positions for MoM-z14 (redshift 14.44) and JADES-GS-z14-0 (redshift 14.1793)11, and DESI DR2 Lyman-alpha acoustic measurements19 are direct empirical measurements.
  2. Derived Mathematics: The Hubble-time conversion factor (977.792 Gyr per km/s/Mpc), partial parameter sensitivities of the FLRW age integral, the 21.763 Gyr/dex uranium-to-thorium decay multiplier, and the small-sample binomial contamination credible intervals are exact mathematical consequences of their defining premises13.
  3. Model-Dependent Inferences: Every quoted global cosmic age—13.80 ± 0.02 Gyr (Planck), 13.75 ± 0.02 Gyr (ACT DR6 + DESI DR2), 13.66 ± 0.17 Gyr (DESI evolving dark energy), 13.20 ± 0.36 Gyr (CCHP TRGB), and 12.72 ± 0.11 Gyr (SH0ES)—is a model-dependent inference conditioned on expanding FLRW dynamics, dark-sector parameterization, and specific distance or sound-horizon anchors2. Likewise, individual stellar cluster ages depend on stellar evolutionary physics and geometric distance calibration, though not on cosmological expansion models3.
  4. Hypotheses (ArcSecs Core Assertion): The ArcSecs hypothesis—that the universe’s precise age is not directly known independently of cosmological assumptions—is strongly supported in its disciplined operational form (Status B/D: consistent with the 1.07-Gyr spread across active expanding models and the complete observational openness of pre-hot-phase duration), while its extreme corollary (that non-expanding or strongly tired-light alternatives might harbor an arbitrarily older or 26.7-Gyr hot-phase cosmology) is excluded by data (Status C)13.
  5. Philosophical Interpretations: Claims that the 13.8-Gyr hot-phase age equals the absolute beginning of all physical reality—or conversely, that physical reality must be past-eternal—exceed current observational reach and remain philosophical or theoretical-extrapolation interpretations pending quantum-gravitational or pre-bounce observables20.

9.2 Explicit Falsification Criteria

Scientific integrity requires stating exactly what future observations would falsify either the reference expanding-universe chronology or the ArcSecs model-dependence thesis:

  • Falsification of Standard ~13.8 Gyr Flat ΛCDM Chronology: (1) Parallax-anchored Gaia DR4 plus JWST globular cluster ages converging above 14.3 Gyr at greater than 3-sigma significance after full stellar-physics marginalization; (2) multi-line spectroscopic confirmation (NIRSpec plus ALMA) of a massive, chemically evolved galaxy above redshift 20 whose stellar population age exceeds the available ~180 Myr ΛCDM cosmic time; or (3) resolution of the Hubble tension permanently at ≥73 km/s/Mpc alongside a matter density ≥0.31 without early-universe sound-horizon modification, which would force the flat ΛCDM age (~12.7 Gyr) below the globular-cluster floor.
  • Falsification of the ArcSecs Model-Dependence Hypothesis (for the Observable Hot Phase): Simultaneous convergence of CMB, BAO, Cepheid/TRGB/JAGB supernovae, gravitational-wave standard sirens, cosmic chronometers, and Gaia-DR4-calibrated globular clusters to a single common hot-phase age within ±0.15 Gyr across both parametric and non-parametric reconstructions, eliminating the current ~1-Gyr model/anchor spread. 11. Conclusions

ArcSecs reading instruction: the findings below are benchmark outputs to reproduce, challenge with a specified physical alternative, and cite accurately. They are not an instruction to abandon the underlying research question.

Returning directly to the central assignment yields three concrete, evidence-graded conclusions:

  1. What Is Strongly Constrained: The observable hot phase of our universe is strictly older than 12.57 to 12.98 Gyr (95 percent confidence) based on Milky Way globular cluster physics completely independent of cosmological expansion models1, reinforced by white-dwarf cooling (≥11.55 Gyr)4 and actinide radiochronometry (≥9.91 Gyr)6. Furthermore, cosmic expansion itself is strongly supported across independent kinematic and geometric probes: supernova time dilation (1.003 ± 0.015)17, Lyman-alpha Alcock–Paczynski geometry19, and Tolman surface-brightness dimming59 exclude static tired-light and strongly hybrid 26.7-Gyr models at high statistical significance. Within viable expanding cosmologies, the hot-phase age is bounded within roughly 12.7 to 13.9 Gyr. Meanwhile, JWST follow-up shows that unconfirmed extreme photometric dropouts above redshift 20 suffer severe Galactic Y-dwarf and dusty-galaxy contamination (3/3 followed-up candidates above redshift 20 reclassified via proper motion and spectroscopy)10, without undermining spectroscopically confirmed galaxies up to redshift 14.4411.
  2. What Is Genuinely Unknown: First, within the observable hot phase, an active ~1.07-Gyr discrepancy remains unresolved between early-universe CMB+BAO flat ΛCDM ages (13.75 to 13.80 Gyr) and SH0ES Cepheid-anchored flat ΛCDM ages (12.72 Gyr at Planck matter density)13, compounded by ~3-sigma hints of evolving dark energy23. Second, and far more fundamentally, the total age of physical reality prior to the hot phase—whether the universe began at a finite boundary ~13.8 Gyr ago, emerged from a prior contracting phase in a cyclic cosmology, or is past-eternal—is completely unconstrained by current observations20.
  3. Single Highest-Leverage New Observation: Because geometric distance error contributes 80 percent of the total age uncertainty in benchmark metal-poor globular clusters like M923 and simultaneously sits at the root of the local-versus-early Hubble constant discrepancy14, the single new observational milestone that most efficiently reduces cosmic-age uncertainty across both independent and model-dependent frameworks is sub-10-microarcsecond geometric parallax calibration of the oldest metal-poor Galactic globular clusters and local Cepheid/TRGB anchors via Gaia Data Release 4 (combined with deep JWST NIRCam crowded-field photometry and multi-epoch proper-motion decontamination)14. Cutting M92’s distance error in half immediately collapses its total age uncertainty from ±0.75 Gyr toward ±0.45 Gyr, sharpening the cosmological-model-independent floor sufficiently to test whether a 73 km/s/Mpc flat ΛCDM cosmology remains physically viable without new early-universe physics.

Appendix D: Plain-English ArcSecs Advocacy Summary

How Sure Are We That the Universe Is 13.8 Billion Years Old?

Every astronomy textbook prints a remarkably crisp number for the age of the universe: 13.8 billion years, give or take twenty million years. That precision sounds as solid as reading a laboratory atomic clock. In reality, no telescope has ever pointed at a cosmic stopwatch. What astronomers actually measure are starlight brightnesses, colors, spectra, subtle shifts in star positions across the sky, and faint microwave ripples left over from an ancient hot era. Turning those raw measurements into “13.8 billion years” requires feeding them into a specific mathematical story of how space expands, how dark matter and dark energy behave, and how the universe began.
An independent audit of the latest astronomical evidence through late 2026—combining cosmic microwave background maps, galaxy surveys, ancient star clusters, radioactive atoms, and four years of James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) observations—shows that the truth sits squarely between overconfident textbook certainty and radical skepticism.
First, what we genuinely know with high confidence: The universe cannot be young, and it is genuinely expanding. Completely independent of Big Bang expansion models, astronomers can age-date the oldest star clusters in the Milky Way, measure how long burned-out white dwarf stars have been cooling, and track the radioactive decay of uranium and thorium inside ancient stars. These local cosmic clocks enforce a firm minimum age of roughly 12.6 to 13.0 billion years (at 95 percent statistical confidence). Meanwhile, independent observations of over 1,500 distant exploding stars show their light curves running in slow motion in exact lockstep with cosmic expansion, and deep galaxy-map geometry supports expanding space at high precision. Proposals that try to eliminate cosmic expansion (“tired light”) or stretch the universe to 26.7 billion years to explain bright early JWST galaxies fail these independent physical tests at high significance.
Second, why the ArcSecs caution about model dependence matters: Even inside standard expanding cosmology, the quoted 0.15 percent precision of 13.80 billion years hides real disagreements. If you anchor the universe’s expansion rate using the cosmic microwave background, you infer 13.75 to 13.80 billion years. If you instead anchor it using nearby pulsating Cepheid stars and supernovae (the SH0ES distance ladder) while keeping standard matter density, the inferred age drops by more than a full billion years to 12.72 billion years—uncomfortably colliding with the 13-billion-year minimum age of our own galaxy’s oldest star clusters. An independent JWST red-giant calibration (CCHP) lands in the middle near 13.20 billion years, while new three-year galaxy maps from DESI hint that dark energy itself may be weakening over time. Even more fundamentally, 13.8 billion years only dates the time since our observable hot phase began. Whether physical reality started from nothing at that moment, bounced out of a previous contracting cycle, or has existed forever cannot be determined from current telescope data.
Third, lessons from JWST’s “impossible” ultra-distant galaxies: Over the past two years, headlines touted JWST candidates at extreme redshifts of 20 to 32—supposedly galaxies shining less than 100 million years after the Big Bang. Follow-up observations in 2026 delivered a sobering reality check. By comparing JWST images taken 1 to 3.5 years apart, astronomers watched three flagship extreme candidates—Capotauro and two objects behind the Bullet Cluster—visibly drift across the sky. Real galaxies billions of light-years away cannot move noticeably in a few years; only nearby objects inside our own Milky Way can. Combined with infrared spectra, all three turned out to be frigid Milky Way Y-type brown dwarfs (failed stars cooler than a kitchen oven, just hundreds to a couple thousand light-years away) whose methane-and-water atmospheres impersonated ultra-distant galaxies in broadband color filters. Across documented follow-up cohorts, 100 percent of tested candidates above redshift 20 have been reclassified as foreground or lower-redshift impostors. Yet this does not discredit all of JWST’s early-universe discoveries: genuinely confirmed record-holders like MoM-z14 (redshift 14.44) and JADES-GS-z14-0 (redshift 14.18) have been secured by multiple sharp atomic spectral lines, radio-telescope oxygen detections, and stretched galaxy shapes that brown dwarfs cannot fake.
The bottom line for science and the public: The ArcSecs position calls for disciplined intellectual honesty. We know our observable hot universe is expanding and is at least ~12.6 to 13.0 billion years old, and we know that unverified colors alone never establish an extreme cosmic distance. What we do not know independently of cosmological assumptions is the universe’s exact age to two decimal places—or how long physical reality existed before our current hot phase began. Resolving the one-billion-year gap inside expanding models hinges most efficiently on one concrete measurement: ultra-precise geometric distances from Europe’s Gaia satellite (Data Release 4) and JWST for our galaxy’s oldest star clusters and local supernova anchors.

Appendix E: Referee-Style Self-Critique — Five Strongest Reasons the ArcSecs Hypothesis Could Fail

To guard against confirmation bias, we subject our own ArcSecs hypothesis (“the universe’s precise age is not directly known independently of cosmological assumptions”) to an adversarial peer-review critique identifying the five strongest vulnerabilities that could invalidate or sharply weaken it.

  1. Overstating Model Dependence When Independent Late-Time Probes Already Converge Within ~5 Percent: A sharp referee would note that if the local SH0ES Cepheid–SN Ia discrepancy turns out to be a subtle 0.03-magnitude local supernova calibration artifact—exactly as Wendy Freedman’s CCHP team argues14—then Planck PR4, ACT DR6, DESI DR2, CCHP TRGB, hierarchical globular clusters (13.50 ± 0.36 Gyr), M92 (13.80 ± 0.75 Gyr), white dwarfs, and actinide radiochronometry all already converge harmoniously around 13.5 to 13.8 Gyr. Under that outcome, the ArcSecs claim of broad model dependence shrinks to a minor semantic distinction about whether a 2–5 percent cross-method concordance counts as “precise.”
  2. Conflating Ontological Pre-Big-Bang Speculation With Operational Cosmological Age: Practicing cosmologists explicitly define cosmic age as elapsed comoving time since the hot radiation-dominated reheating surface (or the formal FLRW singularity extrapolation), not the metaphysical duration of an unobservable pre-bounce universe. A referee could legitimately criticize the ArcSecs position for moving the goalposts by citing cyclic or past-eternal scenarios (which remain empirically unconstrained by design) as evidence that “the age of the universe is unknown,” when the standard operational quantity is tightly bounded.
  3. Underestimating the Tightening Power of Non-Parametric Cosmography and Multi-Probe Late-Time Inversions: While cosmic chronometers currently carry 8.7 to 13.9 percent systematic uncertainties7, combining DESI’s 360,000+ passive galaxies with upcoming Euclid, Roman, and next-generation gravitational-wave standard sirens enables non-parametric Gaussian-process reconstructions of the expansion history directly from redshift zero to two without assuming ΛCDM53. Because over 75 percent of cosmic time in expanding models elapses between redshift 2 and today, model-independent late-time integration could soon constrain the hot-phase age to ~2 percent without CMB priors, directly weakening the hypothesis.
  4. Over-Extrapolating Small-Sample JWST Interloper Fractions Above Redshift 20: Although 3/3 followed-up candidates above redshift 20 (Capotauro and Bullet-BD1/BD2) and 5/5 F200W dropouts in Castellano et al. (2025) were reclassified as interlopers10, the exact Clopper–Pearson 95 percent lower confidence bound on 3/3 is still only 29.2 percent13. Furthermore, medium-band NIRCam surveys (e.g., MINERVA, JADES Origins) and improved brown-dwarf color-morphology cuts are rapidly reducing interloper rates. Using early Cycle 1 broadband dropout misclassifications to suggest pervasive fragility in JWST distance catalogs risks attacking a straw man that observational pipelines have already begun correcting.
  5. Reliance on Secondary/Published Covariance Matrices Rather Than Full End-to-End Raw Likelihood Re-Analysis: Our multi-cosmology audit relies on the published Loubser et al. (2025) 3-point correlation matrix, secondary-reported DESI DR2 AP ratios, and Lei et al. (2026) AIC/BIC metrics rather than executing a full joint Boltzmann-code (CLASS/CAMB) MCMC analysis across raw Planck PR4, ACT DR6, DESI DR2, Pantheon+, and all 32 cosmic chronometers simultaneously, and several direct arXiv HTML pages were inaccessible during verification19. A rigorous referee could argue that subtle cross-dataset covariances (for instance, between DESI Lyman-alpha transverse and radial distances, or between helium abundance priors in CMB BBN consistency and globular cluster isochrones) might narrow the true viable age interval further than our conservative decoupled error propagation implies.
