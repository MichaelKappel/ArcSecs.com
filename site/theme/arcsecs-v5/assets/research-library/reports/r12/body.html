When Defending a Theory Becomes an Excuse: The ArcSecs Scientific Method

ArcSecs revised advocacy report 03 | 22 November 2026 Source file: ArcSecs_When_Theory_Defense_Endangers_Science(1).md

Editorial position. This is an ArcSecs-side argument. It foregrounds the physical-mechanism and tired-light/relational research thesis rather than providing a balanced presentation of cosmology. Proposed models remain labeled as proposals; measured constraints are retained as the test targets that a serious alternative must beat.

Central argument

The scientific danger is not that the existing mathematics works. It is that defenders can mistake predictive utility for an ultimate physical explanation and stop asking mechanism-level questions.

Predictive success and physical cause

Scientific equations may predict a result without uniquely specifying what exists. The public metaphor of a stretched fabric is a particularly vulnerable example. ArcSecs asks why time, light, gravity, and distance should not be investigated as measurable interactions and instrument readouts rather than intangible substances.

Institutional incentives versus evidence

Research careers and schools of thought can bias which questions receive attention. This is a reason to demand preregistered tests and openly published residuals, not to assert widespread deception. Scientific reforms should focus on how claims are evaluated, not imagined motives.

Clock readings are not ontology

The measurements of moving and gravity-affected clocks are real. A clock counts physical cycles. ArcSecs proposes to investigate a deeper process-based model, which will earn credibility only if the same dynamics predicts both gravity-related and motion-related differences, and the behavior of particles that are not atomic clocks.

Redshift as an interaction audit

The signal reaching Earth carries less energy than an identified emission feature in a redshifted galaxy. The project should ask what cumulative interaction could contribute. Its explanations must include the source spectrum, energy transfer, event timing, image sharpness, and cosmic abundance data. This is the test-driven application of the philosophical thesis.

The revision criterion

For every model change, record whether the new term predicts something not yet measured. A fitted curve can be exploratory but does not establish predictive progress. Apply the same standard to ArcSecs itself—precisely because its goal is to challenge protected theory.

Proposed decisive tests

  1. Record predictions before gathering the next dataset.
  2. Publish parameter count and uncertainty with each model version.
  3. Require cross-domain physical mechanism instead of separate exceptions.
  4. Invite an independent replication of the proposed novel effect.

Corrections made to the source report

  • Kept the critical thesis without attributing intentional deceit to named scientists.
  • Clarified that clocks record physical effects that still need quantitative explanation.
  • Separated scientific incompleteness from claims that relativity experiments did not occur.

When Scientists Start Acting Like Compulsive Liars: How Protecting a Theory Can Endanger Real Science

An ArcSecs editorial on scientific bias, mathematical models, physical explanations, and the discipline of test-driven discovery

The problem is not intelligence. It is becoming unable to question your own explanation.

When someone is determined to defend a story, every contradiction seems to demand another explanation. An inconvenient fact appears; a qualification is added. Another fact appears; an exception is invented. Before long, the supporting explanations can become more elaborate than the original story.

That pattern is familiar in human behavior. It can appear in scientific reasoning, too—not because physicists are inherently dishonest, but because highly educated people are still people. They have intellectual commitments, professional reputations, students, grants, colleagues, and decades of work invested in ideas they believe are right.

The important question is not whether a scientist has ever revised a theory. Revision is how good science works. The important question is whether each revision risks a new prediction or merely protects an old conclusion from an uncomfortable observation.

There is evidence that this danger is real. In a randomized peer-review experiment published in 2010, reviewers examined manuscripts with identical methods but different reported outcomes. A positive-result version received publication recommendations from 97.3 percent of reviewers, versus 80.0 percent for a no-difference version. Reviewers also rated the identical methodology more favorably and found fewer errors when the results were positive. This does not prove that physicists are lying. It demonstrates something more subtle: even professional evaluators can judge evidence unevenly when outcomes differ. [1]

That is the meaning of the provocative comparison in this headline. Theory protection can start to resemble the endless rationalization of someone defending a false story, even when every participant believes they are acting responsibly.

A correct calculation is not automatically a correct account of physical cause

ArcSecs begins with a straightforward distinction: an observation, a mathematical description, and an underlying physical mechanism are not the same thing.

A navigation map accurately describes roads, distances, and turns. The map does not physically move the vehicle. A mathematical model can likewise describe a phenomenon accurately without establishing that every element of the model is an independently existing physical substance.

This distinction matters in debates about spacetime. Relativity combines spatial and temporal relationships into an exceptionally successful mathematical framework. But it does not logically follow from that success alone that spacetime must be a literal material fabric. Physicists themselves differ on how its geometry should be interpreted.

The popular image of space stretching like rubber can help explain calculations. It can also mislead readers into thinking a metaphor has been directly observed. A useful picture of reality is not necessarily the mechanism of reality.

This criticism does not establish that Einstein’s equations are bad. It establishes a research obligation: if we want to know what physically causes a measurement, we must investigate causes rather than declaring a mathematical description to be the final word.

There is an instructive example inside established physics. The teleparallel equivalent of general relativity describes gravitational dynamics using torsion rather than the usual curvature-based formulation, yet reproduces the same field equations. This does not prove that all geometry can be discarded, or that ArcSecs is experimentally validated. It shows that a successful collection of observations does not uniquely dictate one mathematical presentation of gravity. [2]

Clocks: what is actually measured?

A clock counts physical cycles or events. An atomic clock uses transitions between quantum energy states. Different clocks can accumulate different readings, and precise experiments confirm both gravitational and motion-dependent effects.

That is the observation. A clock is not an instrument that detects a visible fluid called time.

ArcSecs asks whether changes in the processes operating inside clocks could provide a deeper physical account of the observed differences, rather than interpreting the measurements as evidence for a literally malleable temporal substance. Its research proposes tests across different clock architectures and controlled gravitational and velocity conditions.

But here the distinction between asking a good question and answering it matters. NIST experiments have measured motion-related clock differences even at speeds below ten meters per second, separately from height-related differences. Elementary-particle lifetime measurements also reveal velocity-dependent behavior that cannot be explained solely by changes in atomic electron oscillations. [3]

Therefore, a gravity-only explanation of clock discrepancies does not currently pass the full test suite. If ArcSecs proposes a broader interaction-based model, it must explain both motion and gravity, using the same rules across different kinds of physical processes.

We should neither pretend that the clock measurements are imaginary nor claim those measurements prove that time is a material substance. A new physical interpretation earns credibility by predicting the observed numbers, not by denying them.

Black holes: a mathematical boundary is not a physical explanation

Black holes demonstrate why a theory’s boundaries deserve attention.

At an event horizon, an infinity in one familiar coordinate system is not an infinite physical time experienced by a falling observer. Other coordinates remove that apparent divergence. Calling that particular coordinate issue a disproof of relativity would be a mistake.

The deeper problem lies in the classical description of central singularities, where the theory reaches a limit and cannot supply a complete account of the physical state. That is a legitimate foundational question.

A common-sense investigation asks: is an infinite mathematical result telling us something about nature, or telling us that the model has reached the end of its reliable domain?

ArcSecs explores finite compact objects, relational interactions, and alternatives to treating spacetime curvature as the ultimate causal story. Those ideas are legitimate candidates for investigation. Yet they must reproduce observed black-hole shadows, orbital behavior, light bending, and gravitational-wave signals.

The newly reviewed ArcSecs research reports identify a serious issue here: simple Newtonian light deflection supplemented by an intrinsically massive photon is not presently a satisfactory replacement for the precision tests of gravitational lensing. Photon rest mass remains unconfirmed and tightly constrained. A photon can possess energy and momentum without having nonzero rest mass.

This does not end the search for a deeper model. It tells us which proposed shortcuts need revision, and it prevents us from doing what we criticize in others: declaring an attractive mechanism correct before it survives the evidence.

The real scientific opportunity is to explain why the successful predictions work and where the existing theory stops, without mistaking a mathematical infinity for a completed physical description.

Redshift: observing the messenger is not the same as observing its whole journey

Light from distant galaxies arrives redshifted. That is a measured property of the arriving signal.

The standard cosmological interpretation accounts for this through an expanding cosmic geometry. ArcSecs asks whether physical changes during photon propagation could contribute: interactions, energy transfer, altered propagation, or changing physical couplings.

That is a meaningful question because measurements of arriving signals do not automatically reveal every process that acted during their travel.

But a proposed energy-loss mechanism must answer difficult questions. Where does the energy go? How is momentum conserved? Why are distant galaxies not blurred? Why do distant supernova light curves appear stretched by the specific amount expected from expansion? The 2024 Dark Energy Survey study of 1,504 Type Ia supernovae measured a stretching relationship extremely close to the expansion prediction. A simple static tired-light model without a pulse-stretching mechanism does not explain that result. [4]

These requirements should not be dismissed as unnecessary mathematics. They are the real-world regression tests any replacement mechanism has to pass.

The right ArcSecs challenge is sharper than saying expansion has already been disproven: show exactly which part of observed redshift can be generated by a specified physical process, and show that the same mechanism also survives the independent timing, brightness, image-sharpness, and background-radiation tests.

Dark matter and dark energy: evidence is not the same as identification

Modern cosmology infers most of its energy inventory from phenomena not accounted for by ordinary visible matter alone. The underlying nature of dark matter and dark energy remains unresolved.

Those gaps matter. They are proper subjects for competing physical explanations, not articles of faith. But they are not fair to describe as ingredients invented without evidence: dark matter is supported by multiple independent gravitational and cosmological observations; dark energy names an observed pattern of apparent acceleration under the standard cosmological model.

And these should not all be blamed directly on special relativity or even on Einstein’s gravitational equations. General relativity, assumptions about large-scale cosmic geometry, and the six-parameter Lambda-CDM model are distinct layers of explanation.

ArcSecs’ model-complexity dossier identifies promising lines of questioning: the tight relationship between ordinary matter and galaxy rotation, the discrepancy in predicted primordial lithium, the ongoing Hubble-constant disagreement, and the risk that added dark-energy parameters may improve a fit without earning their extra complexity through new predictive successes.

Yet that same dossier reaches a finding we should not conceal: none of the ArcSecs-inspired alternatives it examined currently gives a simpler, quantitatively successful explanation of the entire established cosmological dataset.

This makes the research challenge concrete. Rather than announcing that dark matter and dark energy have been eliminated, demonstrate how a single simpler physical mechanism accounts for several previously separate observations, with fewer free adjustments and successful predictions outside the data used to design it.

When revision becomes an alibi

Philosopher of science Imre Lakatos described research programs that protect a central theoretical commitment while revising surrounding assumptions. Such revisions can be healthy. When they produce unexpected, independently confirmed predictions, the research program progresses. When revisions mostly explain yesterday’s problems after the fact and add no successful new predictions, the program risks degenerating. [5]

That provides an objective way to examine the compulsive-lying analogy.

Healthy revision says: “Here is our changed explanation. Here is the new observation it predicts. Here is what would prove us wrong.”

Defensive revision says: “Here is why the old explanation is still right. If another contradiction appears, we will find another reason.”

Of course real cases are rarely so simple. Instruments malfunction, observational uncertainties matter, and it can be sensible to keep testing a productive model before abandoning it. But the distinction between predicting and merely accommodating is essential.

The danger becomes especially great when a scientist’s professional identity fuses with a framework. Evidence contrary to that framework may begin to feel like an attack on their life’s work. A new explanation then receives unequal scrutiny, not because someone intentionally fabricates results, but because people are exceptionally skilled at defending what they already believe.

Scientific honesty demands awareness of that human tendency. So does intellectual humility from alternative researchers, who are vulnerable to the very same bias.

Common sense is a question generator—not a substitute for measurement

ArcSecs is right to insist that scientific descriptions remain physically intelligible. Asking what a clock actually counts, what a signal actually measures, or why an equation generates an infinity can reveal hidden assumptions.

But common sense has limits. The universe is under no obligation to behave like everyday experience. Some mathematical complications arise because physical systems really are complicated, not because scientists are covering up a mistake.

The solution is not to replace equations with intuition. It is to make every equation answer to the physical observation it is supposed to describe, while demanding that every proposed mechanism make explicit predictions.

Removing a mathematical expression from an article can make the argument easier to communicate. Removing the mathematical test from a scientific theory would make the argument weaker.

ArcSecs can make the deepest contribution by insisting on both: a comprehensible physical account and an uncompromising numerical test.

The ArcSecs test: no protected theories, including our own

Imagine a public theory-accountability ledger. For each disputed phenomenon, it records what was actually measured; what each model predicted before seeing that measurement; which extra assumptions were added; whether those additions made new predictions; which observations corroborated them; and what would falsify the revised explanation.

Run that ledger against established cosmology and against ArcSecs.

If an ArcSecs clock model cannot explain a motion-only experiment, record the failure.

If a massive-photon mechanism conflicts with lensing or photon-mass limits, record the failure.

If a tired-light mechanism predicts redshift but not supernova light-curve stretching, record the failure.

If a mainstream cosmology extension adds parameters merely to fit existing discrepancies without new testable successes, record that too.

Then revise the models and run the same independent tests again—without quietly changing the success criteria.

The winner should not be the model with the oldest reputation, the loudest defenders, the prettiest mathematics, or the easiest verbal story. It should be the model that explains the most observations with the fewest unsupported assumptions and the strongest independently successful predictions.

This is what test-driven development contributes to science. Not the belief that every error means the whole program must be thrown away, but a disciplined separation of genuine repairs from patches that hide deeper defects.

Conclusion: the universe does not care whose life work is at stake

Scientists are not immune to confirmation bias. Their institutions are not immune to inertia. Their theories are not immune to mistakes. And alternative researchers are not immune either.

A scientist’s responsibility is not to defend yesterday’s explanation forever. It is to discover which parts of that explanation survive evidence, which parts need revision, and which may eventually be replaced by a deeper physical account.

ArcSecs’ challenge is therefore not that Einstein must be wrong because his theory is old. It is that no mathematical framework, however successful, should be promoted from a useful description to unquestionable physical reality without further scrutiny.

Ask what was measured. Ask what physically caused it. Demand a prediction that could fail. Compare the explanation with its rivals. Count the hidden assumptions. Publish the failures as well as the successes.

When explanations keep expanding merely to protect the foundation beneath them, science risks adopting the same pattern as someone who cannot stop defending a story.

Real science begins when the search for physical truth becomes more important than protecting the theory that made us famous.
