### **3.1 Core Operational Definitions**

An **event horizon** in standard mathematical models of idealized stationary black holes is the global outer boundary separating spatial locations from which outward-directed electromagnetic signals can eventually escape to arbitrarily distant receivers from locations from which no outward signal ever escapes. As Visser (2014) demonstrated mathematically, verifying whether an event horizon exists at a given location today requires complete knowledge of the entire future mass-energy evolution of the universe into the infinite future; consequently, a global event horizon is a teleological mathematical construction that is generically not physically observable in any finite-duration experiment4.  
An **apparent horizon** or **trapping boundary**, by contrast, is a quasi-local physical boundary defined at a given instant of universal progression parameter T as the outermost closed surface on which locally outward-directed light rays cease to expand outward in cross-sectional area and are driven inward by the local gravitational field5. Because it depends solely on local mass-energy distribution and field gradients at that epoch rather than on the infinite future, a trapping boundary is in principle quasi-locally testable via finite-baseline tidal and optical measurements5. For a static, non-rotating compact mass, this trapping boundary lies at twice the gravitational length scale.  
The **photon sphere** (unstable photon orbit) is the spherical shell around a non-rotating compact mass—located at three gravitational length scales, or one-and-a-half times the trapping radius—where inward tangential gravitational deflection exactly balances a photon's straight-line inertia, permitting unstable circular photon orbits2. Rays grazing just outside this shell can orbit the compact mass one or more half-turns before escaping toward distant telescopes, generating exponentially narrower higher-order lensed subrings42. Around a rotating compact mass, this single spherical shell broadens into a band of prograde and retrograde spherical photon orbits whose radii depend on viewing inclination and azimuthal angle relative to the spin axis18.  
The **black-hole shadow** (critical capture silhouette) is the central dark brightness depression projected on a distant observer's sky plane, corresponding to backward-traced detector lines of sight that terminate inside the trapping boundary rather than passing through surrounding luminous plasma16. Around a non-rotating compact mass, the critical impact parameter marking the shadow edge is approximately 5.196 gravitational length scales, yielding a critical shadow diameter of approximately 10.392 gravitational length scales—roughly 2.6 times wider than the diameter of the trapping boundary itself2. Compact-object rotation shifts this critical capture diameter by less than roughly 4 percent across spin rates and viewing angles18.  
**Accretion emission** denotes broadband electromagnetic radiation—principally millimeter and submillimeter synchrotron emission from hot magnetized electrons, alongside coronal X-rays and fluorescent disk reflection—emitted by plasma orbiting outside and near the trapping boundary30. **Local probe clock count** (corresponding operationally to what conventional literature calls "proper detector time") is the integer number of physical oscillation cycles accumulated by an actual material atomic clock traveling with a probe between two physically marked spatial events; under constant universal T, variations in this cycle count relative to a distant clock arise from how local gravitational field strength and kinetic velocity physically modify atomic transition frequencies, not from time itself stretching. A **distant receiving clock** is a terrestrial or orbital atomic frequency standard (such as the hydrogen masers installed at EHT VLBI stations or Deep Space Network ground clocks used in Cassini tracking) that timestamps arriving wave crests or photon detections in universal T6. **Redshift** is the directly measured fractional reduction in received photon frequency (or increase in wavelength) compared against laboratory transition frequencies of the same atomic species, such as the 2.167-micrometer hydrogen line tracked in star S2 near Sagittarius A\*36. **Signal arrival** is a discrete physical energy-transfer event at a receiver—such as a correlated radio voltage across an interferometric baseline or an X-ray photoelectron registered in a detector—at a specific distant-clock timestamp16.

### **3.2 Why the Visible EHT Ring Is Not a Photograph of a Surface Crossing**

A persistent popular misconception—directly relevant to the coordinate-infinity audit—treats Event Horizon Telescope images of M87\* and Sagittarius A\* as photographs of matter frozen at an event horizon. Primary EHT papers explicitly show otherwise16. First, an EHT image is not a single-frame optical photograph; it is an interferometric synthesis reconstructed from sparse Fourier-plane visibility amplitudes and closure phases recorded across globally separated radio observatories at 1.3-millimeter wavelength (230 gigahertz), yielding a nominal angular resolution of roughly 20 to 25 microarcseconds16. Second, the bright ring arises from optically thin magnetized synchrotron plasma orbiting outside the trapping boundary, heavily shaped by gravitational light bending and relativistic Doppler beaming30. Third, the dark interior depression (contrast ratio at least 10 to 1 in M87\*) spans roughly 42 microarcseconds in M87\* and 51.8 microarcseconds in Sagittarius A\*, tracing the critical photon-capture impact parameter (roughly 5.2 gravitational length scales in radius), which is approximately 2.6 times wider than the inferred trapping boundary (two gravitational length scales in radius)16. Nothing in EHT visibility data captures a photon or infalling body paused at a surface crossing; rather, the central darkness marks a deficit of escaping 230-gigahertz photons along trajectories that fall inside the gravitational capture threshold18.

### **4.1 Horizon-Scale Radio Interferometry: M87\* and Sagittarius A\***

During four nights in April 2017 (April 5, 6, 10, and 11), the Event Horizon Telescope linked eight millimeter-wavelength radio stations across six global sites (baselines from 160 meters to 10,700 kilometers) observing at 1.3-millimeter wavelength (230 gigahertz)16. Across independent calibration and imaging pipelines, M87\* was resolved as an asymmetric circular bright ring with a diameter of 42 ± 3 microarcseconds encircling a central brightness depression of at least 10 to 116. Using an adopted distance of 16.8 ± 0.8 megaparsecs, the EHT collaboration derived an angular gravitational scale of 3.8 ± 0.4 microarcseconds and a central mass of 6.5 ± 0.7 billion solar masses16. Follow-up April 2018 observations confirmed the persistence of the ring with a median diameter of 43.3 \+1.5 / −3.1 microarcseconds while revealing that the azimuthal brightness peak shifted by approximately 30 degrees from the southeast toward the southwest—consistent with turbulent accretion variability around a spin axis aligned roughly with M87\*'s large-scale jet (inclined roughly 17 degrees from the line of sight)18. Subsequent multi-epoch EHT analysis spanning 2017 through 2021 (published December 2025\) tracked continued structural and polarimetric variability around a persistent ring diameter31.  
Multi-frequency VLBI now brackets the 230-gigahertz EHT band from both sides. At 86 gigahertz (3.5-millimeter wavelength), Lu et al. (2023) used the Global Millimeter VLBI Array (GMVA) together with phased ALMA and the Greenland Telescope to resolve a thicker, edge-brightened ring-like accretion structure in M87\* with a reported diameter of 64 microarcseconds—roughly 50 percent larger than the 230-gigahertz ring—directly linking the inner accretion flow to the launching base of the collimated jet34. At higher frequency (345 gigahertz, 870-micrometer wavelength), Raymond et al. (2024) achieved the first ground-based VLBI detections from Earth's surface, verifying station sensitivity and signal-chain stability at wavelengths corresponding to roughly 19-microarcsecond nominal diffraction resolution33.  
In May 2022, the EHT released its first horizon-scale analysis of Sagittarius A\* from the 2017 campaign29. Despite strong intrahour structural variability and interstellar scattering along the Galactic plane, independent imaging and modeling pipelines converged on a bright, thick ring with a diameter of 51.8 ± 2.3 microarcseconds (68 percent credible interval) surrounding a comparatively dim central interior, while disfavoring non-spinning configurations, retrograde accretion disks, and high viewing inclinations exceeding 50 degrees17. Crucially, unlike M87\*, Sagittarius A\* possesses high-precision independent mass and distance measurements derived from decades of near-infrared stellar orbit tracking at scales of roughly a thousand gravitational length scales50. GRAVITY Collaboration (2022) measured a central mass of 4.297 ± 0.012 million solar masses (statistical, with roughly 40,000 solar masses systematic) and a distance of 8,277 ± 9 parsecs (statistical, with roughly 30 parsecs systematic)19, building on earlier 20-sigma detections of the S2 pericenter gravitational redshift (a 200-kilometer-per-second residual) by GRAVITY (2018) and Keck (Do et al. 2019), plus the 5-sigma measurement of prograde S2 orbital precession of 12.1 arcminutes per revolution (precession scaling factor 1.10 ± 0.19)11. Confronting the EHT 2022 ring size against those independent priors yielded a fractional shadow-diameter deviation of minus 0.08 \+0.09 / −0.09 using the VLTI prior and minus 0.04 \+0.09 / −0.10 using the Keck prior—demonstrating mass-scaling consistency across more than three orders of magnitude in central mass10. Moreover, observed infrared upper limits on Sagittarius A\* are incompatible with models in which infalling matter strikes a thermalizing solid or fluid surface rather than crossing an absorbing trapping boundary11.  
Horizon-scale polarimetry provides an independent constraint on magnetic fields and electromagnetic propagation near compact objects. In M87\* (EHT Papers VII and VIII, 2021), the 230-gigahertz ring exhibits a coherent, largely azimuthal linear polarization pattern evolving over four days, consistent with dynamically important magnetic fields and an inferred accretion rate near one Jupiter mass per year (as summarized in AAS Nova coverage of Papers VII and VIII)63. In Sagittarius A\* (EHT Papers VII and VIII, 2024), the 230-gigahertz ring displays a prominent spiral electric vector polarization angle pattern with a peak linear polarization fraction near 40 percent in the western portion of the ring32. Complementing radio polarimetry, GRAVITY Collaboration (2023) measured near-infrared flare astrometry and polarization-angle loops around Sagittarius A\* on horizon scales, while Daly et al. (2023) derived an outflow-based dimensionless spin estimate of 0.90 ± 0.0635.

Strong-field gravitational deflection of photons is independently recorded at X-ray energies millions of times higher than EHT millimeter radio waves. Wilkins et al. ### **7.3 Controls, Selection Effects, and Astrophysical Systematics (Research Question 5\)**

Idealized textbook diagrams of static, spherically symmetric vacuum horizons diverge substantially from real astrophysical compact objects. Five physical systematics must be explicitly controlled before attributing any horizon-scale image feature to fundamental gravitational physics.

## ArcSecs interpretation-versus-observation checkpoint (editorial addition, R25)

- **Observed or calibrated quantities:** Horizon-scale light and source variability are recorded by telescopes; no astronomical instrument directly times a photon crossing an event horizon.
- **What the observation does not determine by itself:** It does not identify a unique fundamental physical ontology or interaction mechanism; experimental limits must remain explicitly labeled as limits.
- **ArcSecs mechanism-building task:** Separate detector arrival counts, source-frame process duration, and coordinate singularity; develop a finite physical capture law without claiming an infinitesimal crossing as measured.
- **Reproducible comparison / sources:** Event Horizon Telescope M87* and Sgr A* visibility products, X-ray timing and ringdown data.
- **Falsification rule:** Retain every published measured quantity and uncertainty. If a specified ArcSecs mechanism fails that benchmark, record the failing assumption and design a more complete physical mechanism or a discriminating observation; do not erase or recast the measurement as a successful fit.
- **Scientific release status:** Research working edition. Source bibliography, numerical calculations, author attribution and any claimed original simulations require independent scientific verification before journal submission.
