2. Physical argument and mechanism candidates

Astronomers directly measure Doppler shifts, stellar trajectories, gas dynamics and lensing patterns. Assigning these effects to a particular dark-matter species or dark-energy field is an inference made within a model. ArcSecs is justified in demanding a physical inventory rather than a label. Galaxy rotation offers a promising arena because baryonic structure and total acceleration display precise regularities in SPARC observations. The challenge is to extend the same law to cluster mergers, CMB structure and cosmic evolution without adding a new adjustable halo for each difficult case.

The Dark Matter Drive is a distinct engineering investigation. Even if there is a stable dark population, a spacecraft cannot harvest it merely because it passes through the hull. The apparatus must exert a force on the medium, acquire momentum or mass, and produce a net reaction thrust after aerodynamic or collection drag. Standard estimated local halo dark matter density is about 0.3 GeV per cubic centimeter, corresponding to approximately 5.35×10^-22 kg/m³; it is not 0.01 kg/m³. At that density an idealized scoop of area 1 m² passing at 0.1c meets only about 1.6×10^-14 kg/s of nominal mass-equivalent flux before any capture penalty. This is an upper bookkeeping estimate, not a feasible collection rate.

A candidate new coupling may still be worth developing, but it must predict laboratory interactions or astronomical drag. The drive is not evidence for the substrate; verified substrate physics is the prerequisite for a drive.

1. Introduction

Modern astrophysics rests on precision observations spanning more than fifteen orders of magnitude in spatial scale, from wide binary star orbits and neutral-hydrogen disk rotation curves to colliding galaxy clusters, cosmic microwave background (CMB) anisotropies, and gigaparsec-scale baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)1. Within the prevailing concordance model—flat Lambda Cold Dark Matter (ΛCDM), built upon General Relativity (GR) with a spatially homogeneous and isotropic Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) background metric—these phenomena are unified by introducing two dark components: a cold, effectively collisionless non-baryonic dark-matter fluid accounting for 26.4% of the critical density (84.4% of total matter), and a spatially uniform cosmological constant driving late-time accelerated expansion6.
Despite its broad phenomenological success, the dark sector remains operationally indirect. Through November 22, 2026, no laboratory experiment has confirmed direct detection of a dark-matter particle. The LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) experiment reported null results across 4.2 tonne-years of liquid-xenon exposure in 2025 and explicitly cautioned that a single unusual high-energy outlier event presented in September 2026 at two-to-three-sigma significance relative to expected backgrounds does not constitute a discovery claim13. Meanwhile, high-precision astrophysical observations continue to display striking structural regularities and inter-probe tensions: rotationally supported disk galaxies obey an exceptionally tight coupling between visible baryons and total centripetal acceleration1; early- and late-universe measurements of the Hubble constant remain in active dispute depending on local stellar anchors15; and 2025 DESI BAO releases triggered intense debate over whether dark energy evolves over cosmic time10.
The ArcSecs research position mandates a disciplined test of this paradigm: rather than treating inferred mathematical constructs as literal physical substances prior to exhausting mechanistic explanations, one must formulate explicit competing physical laws, separate raw observables from model-dependent inferences, and confront every alternative with the complete hierarchy of astrophysical data. Crucially, questioning ΛCDM is not equivalent to discarding General Relativity as a whole. General Relativity is a covariant theory of spacetime curvature verified to high precision in solar-system dynamics, binary pulsars, and gravitational-wave propagation—including the near-simultaneous arrival of gravitational waves and electromagnetic radiation from the binary neutron-star merger GW17081719. By contrast, ΛCDM is a specific cosmological model embedded inside GR that combines particular matter-inventory assumptions, a cosmological constant, and Gaussian primordial perturbations. Any competing gravitational or relational mechanism must respect strong-field and solar-system tests of GR while matching or improving upon ΛCDM across extragalactic scales.
A strictly separate question arises from speculation that if a real dark substrate pervades the solar neighborhood, future spacecraft could collect and use it as propellant or fuel. Evaluating that proposal requires insulating engineering physics from cosmological debate. Regardless of whether galactic dynamics reflect particle halos or modified gravity, propulsion feasibility is governed solely by local mass-energy flux, experimentally bounded particle interaction probabilities, momentum conservation (including intake ram drag), thermodynamic heat rejection, and structural mass budgets. This report evaluates astrophysical explanatory power and engineered propulsion feasibility independently, holding each to verified quantitative calculations.

Telescopes never directly observe dark-matter halos, virial concentrations, or dark-energy equations of state; they record photon fluxes, angular positions, spectral Doppler shifts, background galaxy ellipticities, X-ray surface brightness, microwave intensity and polarization Stokes parameters, and standardized supernova light curves. 3. Literature Review Through November 2026

3.1 Galaxy Dynamics, SPARC, and the Radial Acceleration Relation

The premier modern benchmark for disk-galaxy dynamics is the Spitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves (SPARC) database assembled by Lelli, McGaugh, and Schombert (2016)3. Combining homogeneous near-infrared 3.6-micrometer photometry—which traces stellar mass with low dust attenuation and narrow stellar mass-to-light scatter—with published neutral-hydrogen (HI) and H-alpha rotation curves across 175 late-type galaxies spanning five orders of magnitude in stellar mass and more than three orders of magnitude in surface brightness, SPARC enabled McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert (2016) to establish the empirical Radial Acceleration Relation (RAR) across nearly 2,700 individual data points in 153 galaxies1. Above a characteristic acceleration scale near 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared, observed centripetal acceleration tracks the Newtonian acceleration generated solely by visible stars and atomic gas; below that scale, observed acceleration diverges upward in tight one-to-one correspondence with the baryonic mass distribution, with residual scatter largely consistent with observational uncertainties1.
Galaxy-by-galaxy fits to SPARC under physically motivated Gaussian priors on distance, inclination, and stellar mass-to-light ratio (Li et al. 2018) demonstrated that a single universal acceleration scale reproduces most rotation curves, whereas standard two-parameter Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) cold-dark-matter halos exhibit substantially wider fit-quality scatter and frequent boundary-limited solutions3. Hydrodynamical feedback profiles such as DC14, coreNFW, and flexible core-Einasto halos mitigate inner-halo cusp–core discrepancies by adding extra structural freedom3. An August 2026 comparative SPARC benchmark (arXiv:2608.11575) further showed that a single-parameter phenomenological model (Smooth-CCC) achieves a mean reduced chi-squared of 2.58 compared with 2.65 for galaxy-by-galaxy MOND and roughly 7.0 for two-parameter NFW halos, while retaining physically expected stellar mass-to-light ratios near 0.5 to 0.7 where MOND occasionally drives dwarf-galaxy ratios unphysically low3.
Crucially, relational modified-dynamics theories generate untuned out-of-sample predictions absent from standard cold dark matter: the External Field Effect (EFE), whereby an ambient external gravitational field suppresses the low-acceleration boost inside a subsystem, and an approximate 20% orbital velocity boost in Milky Way wide binary star systems separated by 2 to 30 kilo-astronomical units14. Chae et al. (2020) reported statistical evidence for the EFE in SPARC galaxies situated in external fields22. However, Gaia Data Release 3 wide-binary tests remain deeply contested through 2026: Chae (2023, 2024) reported a low-acceleration anomaly favoring MOND with EFE, whereas Banik et al. (2024) and 2025–2026 re-analyses incorporating realistic unresolved hierarchical triple stars find no 20% velocity enhancement and full consistency with Newtonian gravity14.

3.2 Galaxy Clusters and the Bullet Cluster Through 2026

Galaxy clusters have long posed the sternest astrophysical challenge to modified-dynamics models lacking unseen mass. In the Bullet Cluster (1E 0657–558 at redshift 0.296), Clowe et al. (2006) combined Chandra X-ray surface-brightness maps of shock-heated intracluster plasma with weak gravitational lensing reconstructions, establishing an eight-sigma spatial offset between the dominant baryonic gas component stalled near the merger center and the gravitational-lensing mass peaks aligned with the colliding galaxy populations2. Analogous offsets were observed in other supersonic cluster collisions such as MACS J0025.4–122230. Under ΛCDM, electromagnetic ram pressure decelerates colliding gas while collisionless dark matter and stars pass through largely unimpeded, constraining the dark-matter self-interaction cross section per unit mass to less than 0.47 square centimeters per gram (roughly 0.84 barn per GeV at 95% confidence level, per the 2025 Particle Data Group review)2.
Updated James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) NIRCam imaging of the Bullet Cluster (Program GO-4598, PI: M. Bradač) enabled Cha et al. (2025) to produce a refined member-galaxy catalog and General Relativity strong-lensing mass reconstructions across three core regions centered on the Brightest Cluster Galaxies2. A June 2026 re-analysis (arXiv:2606.19454) highlighted two interpretive subtleties: first, because the sound-crossing time exceeds the merger timescale, hot gas departs from hydrostatic equilibrium (and Clowe et al. actually recorded four X-ray peaks, including two smaller peaks near the brightest galaxies); second, applying the Integrated Galaxy-wide Initial Mass Function (IGIMF) framework—which predicts a top-heavy early starburst IMF in metal-rich massive cluster ellipticals and thus abundant dark stellar remnants (white dwarfs, neutron stars, and stellar-mass black holes)—places the baryonic-plus-remnant mass budget inside all three JWST core regions directly between the lower (constant-metallicity) and upper (enriched-metallicity) bounds required by MOND strong lensing, whereas General Relativity still suffers a substantial missing-mass deficit even with IGIMF remnants2. Whether such dark stellar remnants can simultaneously match wide-field weak-lensing shear profiles outside cluster cores and primordial nucleosynthesis limits remains unverified.

3.3 Relativistic Completions, CMB, and Cosmological Constraints (2025–2026)

Non-relativistic MOND possesses no spacetime metric and therefore cannot predict gravitational lensing or CMB acoustic peaks. Earlier relativistic attempts such as Tensor–Vector–Scalar (TeVeS) gravity were ruled out by gravitational-wave speed bounds from GW170817 and poor third-peak CMB fits19. Skordis and Złośnik (2019, 2021) overcame those obstacles with Aether–Scalar–Tensor (AeST) theory, a relativistic gravitational framework whose tensor gravitational waves travel at the exact speed of light, whose quasi-static weak-field potential obeys a non-linear dielectric-like Poisson law recovering galactic MOND, and whose cosmological degrees of freedom reproduce the observed CMB power spectra and linear matter power spectrum7.
Between March 2025 and September 2026, observational cosmology underwent a rapid series of precision updates and critical statistical audits. In March 2025, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Collaboration released Data Release 2 (DR2), analyzing three years of survey data covering more than 14 million galaxies and quasars plus roughly 820,000 Lyman-alpha forests10. DESI DR2 improved combined galaxy/quasar BAO precision 1.7-fold over DR1 to 0.30% (statistical uncertainty ~0.24%) and achieved 0.65% isotropic precision at redshift 2.33 in the Lyman-alpha forest10. Combined with Planck CMB measurements, DESI DR2 reported a mild 2.3-sigma tension in flat ΛCDM and a 3.1-sigma frequentist preference (maximum-a-posteriori chi-squared shift of −12.5, dropping to 2.7 sigma without CMB lensing) for two-parameter evolving dark energy—rising to 2.8 to 4.2 sigma when adding Pantheon+, Union3, or DES-SN5YR supernovae—while simultaneously yielding an ultra-tight ΛCDM neutrino mass bound below 0.0642 eV (95% CL; Feldman–Cousins bound below 0.053 eV) that creates a 3-sigma tension with laboratory oscillation lower limits (0.059 eV) unless dark energy evolves (relaxing the limit to 0.163 eV)10.
Subsequent 2025–2026 releases substantially tempered claims of evolving dark energy. In July 2026, DESI DR2 Results IV analyzed the full correlation-function shape of the Lyman-alpha forest via the Alcock–Paczyński distortion at 1% precision at redshift 2.33, tightening transverse and radial distance ratios to 0.8% precision—and shifting the central value back toward standard ΛCDM10. Concurrently, the Atacama Cosmology Telescope final Data Release 6 (ACT DR6, 2025) combined with Planck and DESI DR2 yielded a Hubble constant of 68.43 ± 0.27 km per second per megaparsec, with P-ACT and DESI DR2 agreeing within 1.6 sigma under ΛCDM18. Efstathiou (2025) rotated DESI BAO ratios into orthogonal geometric projections, showing that DESI DR2 moved into closer agreement with Planck ΛCDM than DR1 along the tightly predicted perpendicular axis while pinning the dark-energy equation of state at pivot redshift 0.5 to −0.996 ± 0.046, and diagnosing a low-redshift photometric mismatch in DES-SN5YR11. A September 2026 out-of-sample Expected Log Pointwise Predictive Density (ELPD) and Bayesian evidence audit (arXiv:2609.04334) confirmed that Bayesian evidence for DESI DR2 + Planck favors ΛCDM (log Bayes factor of +0.57 ± 0.26), that removing the single LRG2 bin at redshift 0.706 eliminates the entire BAO out-of-sample preference for evolving dark energy, and that replacing DES-SN5YR with the recalibrated DES-Dovekie catalog eliminates the Bayesian preference entirely12. On the Hubble tension, SH0ES Cepheid calibrations cross-checked by JWST remain near 73 km per second per megaparsec (Riess et al. 2022, 2024, 2025), whereas the Chicago–Carnegie Hubble Program (Freedman et al. 2025) using JWST Tip of the Red Giant Branch (TRGB) and J-region Asymptotic Giant Branch (JAGB) calibrations differs at the few-percent level, leaving the systematic versus new-physics origin of the tension unresolved15.

3.4 Direct Dark Matter Detection Limits Through November 2026

Experimental constraints on particle dark matter are strictly species-specific and must never be extrapolated universally across distinct particle families. For Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs), the LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) experiment set the strictest spin-independent WIMP–nucleon upper limit of 2.2 × 10⁻⁴⁸ square centimeters at a WIMP mass of 40 GeV per c-squared (90% CL) from 4.2 tonne-years of liquid-xenon exposure (Aalbers et al. 2025), excluding WIMPs above 9 GeV per c-squared nearly five times more tightly than prior searches13. At the September 2026 TeVPA conference, LZ reported a single unusual high-energy outlier event in 220 live days of 2023–2024 data at two-to-three-sigma significance relative to expected backgrounds, explicitly cautioning that it is not a discovery and requires further background vetting16. XENONnT offers comparable multi-tonne sensitivity, with projected spin-dependent limits reaching 2.2 × 10⁻⁴³ square centimeters on neutrons and 6.0 × 10⁻⁴² square centimeters on protons44. For ultra-light bosonic candidates (axions and axion-like particles spanning 10⁻²² to 10⁻² eV), microwave cavity haloscopes such as ADMX and HAYSTAC probe weak electromagnetic couplings6. For millicharged dark matter, CMB and large-scale structure decoupling limits restrict electric charge below 3.5 × 10⁻⁷ of the elementary charge at 1 GeV6.

4.1 Reference Null Model (H0): General Relativity with ΛCDM

Under H0, spacetime curvature obeys Einstein’s field dynamics supplemented by a cosmological constant, sourced by ordinary baryons, photons, neutrinos, and a cold, collisionless non-baryonic dark-matter fluid6. In galaxies, total inward radial acceleration is the linear Newtonian sum of baryonic and dark-matter halo accelerations, requiring at least two free structural parameters per galaxy (such as virial velocity and concentration in an NFW halo, plus additional core-shaping degrees of freedom in feedback-modified profiles like DC14 or Einasto)3. Weak-field gravitational lensing deflection equals twice the Newtonian potential gradient and therefore tracks total gravitating mass regardless of baryonic state. Cosmic expansion follows flat FLRW geometry driven by matter, radiation, and a constant vacuum energy density.

4.2 Competing Hypothesis H1: Relational Acceleration Law (Non-Linear Milgromian Dynamics)

Hypothesis H1 introduces a single modified gravitational potential sourced exclusively by baryonic mass density, governed by a non-linear generalization of Poisson’s field law parameterized by one universal acceleration scale (approximately 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared) and a dimensionless interpolating transition function—mathematically analogous to the electrostatic field inside a non-linear dielectric medium2. No dark-matter degrees of freedom exist.
From this field law follow three direct kinematic and optical consequences. First, regarding radial acceleration, wherever the Newtonian acceleration produced by stars and gas greatly exceeds the universal acceleration scale, the interpolating function approaches unity and inverse-square Newtonian gravity is recovered identically; wherever baryonic Newtonian acceleration drops far below that scale in outer galactic disks, effective radial acceleration transitions smoothly to the geometric mean of the baryonic Newtonian acceleration and the universal scale, declining inversely with galactocentric radius rather than inversely with radius squared. Second, regarding rotation speed, equating circular centripetal acceleration (orbital speed squared divided by radius) to this deep-regime gravitational acceleration cancels radial dependence identically, forcing rotation curves to flatten asymptotically to a constant terminal velocity whose fourth power equals the product of Newton’s gravitational constant, total baryonic mass, and the universal acceleration scale—deriving the empirical Baryonic Tully–Fisher Relation with zero per-galaxy halo parameters1. Third, regarding lensing response, because H1 is a non-relativistic quasi-static potential law, it possesses no intrinsic relativistic light-deflection derivation; if naively coupled to null geodesics via the enhanced baryonic potential, lensing convergence must track the baryonic mass center, which fails wherever dominant X-ray plasma is spatially separated from lensing peaks unless invisible compact baryons reside at those peaks2.
Finally, H1 yields two untuned out-of-sample predictions unavailable to linear Newtonian gravity with dark matter. Because its field law is non-linear, it violates the Strong Equivalence Principle: a gravitating subsystem immersed in an external gravitational field stronger than its internal acceleration experiences a suppressed boost—the External Field Effect (EFE)22. Independently, isolated Milky Way wide binary star pairs separated by more than roughly 5 to 7 kilo-astronomical units experience internal accelerations below the universal scale and should exhibit an approximate 20% statistical boost in relative orbital speed moderated by the Galactic external field14.

4.3 Competing Hypothesis H2: Covariant Aether–Scalar–Tensor Effective Field Theory (AeST)

Hypothesis H2 specifies a covariant relativistic gravitational action coupling the spacetime metric tensor to two auxiliary gravitational degrees of freedom—a unit-norm timelike vector (“aether”) field defining a preferred local rest frame and a dynamical scalar field—governed by dimensionless coupling parameters and a kinetic potential function yielding the galactic acceleration scale7.
In the quasi-static weak-field regime around isolated baryonic disks, spatial gradients of the scalar field satisfy a non-linear dielectric-like field relation equivalent to H1, recovering the identical radial acceleration law, asymptotically flat rotation curves, and Baryonic Tully–Fisher Relation with one universal acceleration scale7. Unlike pure H1, however, AeST derives a consistent relativistic lensing response: light rays travel along null geodesics of the same metric whose weak-field potentials match the enhanced dynamical potential, reproducing galaxy-scale weak gravitational lensing without dark-matter halos while preserving exact light-speed propagation for tensor gravitational waves in agreement with GW17081719. As an untuned cosmological prediction, the homogeneous time-evolving sector of AeST behaves dynamically like pressureless cold dust on cosmic scales, allowing the theory to reproduce the CMB acoustic peak heights and linear matter power spectrum without particle dark matter7. Critically, AeST still retains a cosmological constant to account for late-time cosmic acceleration, and its non-linear behavior in supersonic merging clusters remains an exacting, uncompleted test.

4.4 Competing Hypothesis H3: Non-Dark-Energy Distance–Redshift Imitations

To test whether inferred cosmic acceleration could reflect an altered distance–redshift law rather than dark energy, we evaluate four explicit zero-dark-energy proposals. H3a (Einstein–de Sitter) models a spatially flat, matter-only FLRW universe with unity matter fraction and zero dark energy (one scaling parameter: the product of the Hubble constant and the drag-epoch sound horizon)4. H3b (Open Cold Dark Matter) retains zero dark energy while allowing negative spatial curvature to compensate for sub-critical matter density (two free parameters)4. H3c (Flat Coasting / Logarithmic Tired Light) imposes zero cosmic acceleration—yielding a logarithmic transverse distance–redshift mapping geometrically identical to an idealized logarithmic photon-energy-loss model (one scaling parameter)—alongside an Open Milne empty coasting metric and a Euclidean Static Linear Tired-Light mapping where distance scales linearly with redshift4. H3d (Inhomogeneous Backreaction / Timescape Cosmology) posits that averaging GR over cosmic voids and filaments induces differential gravitational clock rates that mimic cosmic acceleration without vacuum energy50.

4.5 Competing Hypothesis H4: Engineered Dark-Sector Substrate Capture and Propulsion

Conditional on a real particulate dark-matter halo pervading the solar neighborhood at the standard astrophysical energy-density convention, H4 posits that a spacecraft deploying an engineered interaction volume or field scoop of cross-sectional area, collection length, and capture efficiency can harvest ambient dark-matter flux at Galactic orbital velocities and generate net forward thrust exceeding intake ram drag, ambient interstellar gas drag, and structural mass penalties.

6.2 Galaxy Dynamics and SPARC Benchmark Evaluation

On galaxy scales, the SPARC compilation provides the sharpest empirical comparison between dark-matter halos (H0) and one-parameter relational acceleration laws (H1 and the quasi-static limit of H2). Across nearly 2,700 radial measurements in 153 SPARC galaxies, observed centripetal acceleration correlates tightly with the Newtonian acceleration computed solely from Spitzer 3.6-micrometer starlight and HI gas, bending systematically away from Newtonian expectations below roughly 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ meters per second squared1. Localized photometric bumps such as spiral arms produce matching bumps in rotation curves, binding dynamics directly to visible baryons even where standard models invoke overwhelming dark-matter dominance1.
Because raw per-galaxy SPARC tables were not locally executed (preregistered 70/30 split not executed), we evaluate published galaxy-by-galaxy SPARC fit comparisons from Li et al. (2018) and the August 2026 comparative benchmark (arXiv:2608.11575)3. Those published metrics expose a stark contrast between one-parameter relational economy and two-parameter NFW dark-matter halos.

Two important analytical caveats bound this galaxy-scale advantage. First, although pure two-parameter NFW halos perform poorly—with 31 of 165 fits hitting boundary constraints (25 at minimal concentration and 7 at maximum virial velocity of 600 km per second)—baryonic feedback in hydrodynamical simulations transforms inner dark-matter cusps into cores (modeled via DC14, coreNFW, and core-Einasto profiles), improving ΛCDM fits at the cost of extra structural parameters3. Second, MOND itself displays nuisance-parameter strain on individual dwarfs: on IC 2574, matching observations under free stellar mass-to-light priors drives the MOND disk mass-to-light ratio down to 0.2 solar masses per solar luminosity (echoing 0.07 in Li et al. 2018), well below the physical stellar population expectation near 0.5 to 0.73.
Equally decisive is the contested status of H1’s untuned out-of-sample predictions through November 2026. While Chae et al. (2020) reported statistical support for the External Field Effect in SPARC rotation curves22, the cleanest local test—Milky Way wide binary stars in Gaia DR3—yields conflicting verdicts across research teams. Banik et al. (2024) and 2025–2026 analyses incorporating realistic unresolved hierarchical triple stars find no 20% velocity boost across 2 to 30 kilo-astronomical units and report consistency with Newtonian gravity, whereas Chae (2023, 2024) maintains that stricter sample-cleaning selections retain a low-acceleration MOND signal14. Accordingly, we classify H1’s disk rotation-curve performance as Code B (parsimonious superiority over basic NFW, partially matched by feedback-modified halos) and its untuned wide-binary test as Code D (empirically undecidable pending consensus on triple-star contamination).

6.3 Galaxy Clusters and Merging Clusters: The Bullet Cluster Test

Stepping up from individual galaxies to rich galaxy clusters exposes the sharpest limitation of purely baryonic relational gravity. On cosmic and cluster scales, dark matter in benchmark ΛCDM accounts for 84.4% of total matter (roughly 5.4 times the baryonic mass fraction)6, and even after applying the MOND low-acceleration boost, canonical baryonic cluster budgets have historically left a substantial residual missing-mass discrepancy2.
The merging Bullet Cluster (1E 0657–558, redshift 0.296) converts that mass deficit into a geometric separation test2. Because hot X-ray-emitting plasma constitutes the vast majority of known cluster baryons, a local modified-gravity law sourced solely by visible baryons predicts gravitational-lensing peaks centered on the stalled central gas clouds. Instead, Chandra X-ray and weak-lensing observations (Clowe et al. 2006) revealed an eight-sigma spatial offset placing the dominant lensing convergence peaks over the flanking collisionless galaxy groups—a morphology independently replicated in MACS J0025.4–12222.

Updated 2025–2026 JWST reconstructions refine this test without rescuing pure visible-baryon modified gravity. Analyzing the JWST/NIRCam strong- and weak-lensing maps of Cha et al. (2025, Program GO-4598), a June 2026 study (arXiv:2606.19454) demonstrated that unseen collisionless mass co-located with the cluster galaxies remains mandatory even under MOND2. The distinction lies in the required nature and quantity of that unseen mass: under General Relativity, augmenting luminous stars and intracluster light with dark stellar remnants (white dwarfs, neutron stars, and stellar-mass black holes) predicted by the IGIMF framework for early metal-rich starbursts still leaves a severe mass deficit requiring genuine non-baryonic dark matter; within MOND, by contrast, the measured strong-lensing masses across all three Brightest Cluster Galaxy core regions fall directly between the lower (constant-metallicity) and upper (enriched-metallicity) IGIMF baryonic-plus-remnant mass limits, alongside non-hydrostatic uncertainties in shocked X-ray gas masses2.
Consequently, the Bullet Cluster does not leave pure baryonic H1 intact: pure visible-baryon H1 is excluded (Code C) in merging clusters. Remnant-augmented H1/H2 narrows the cluster discrepancy to whether the required unseen collisionless mass consists of non-baryonic cold dark matter or compact dark stellar remnants, a hypothesis classified as Code D until wide-field weak-lensing shear profiles outside the three galaxy cores, relaxed cluster mass functions, and Big Bang Nucleosynthesis baryon-density constraints are jointly verified.

6.5 Audit of the Hubble Tension and 2025–2026 Evolving Dark Energy Claims

Understanding why combined 2025 analyses nevertheless reported 2.8-to-4.2-sigma frequentist preferences for evolving dark energy—including an apparent high-redshift phantom crossing—requires auditing four interacting statistical and systematic mechanisms. First, the apparent preference originates primarily from a mild inter-probe parameter offset rather than internal BAO curvature: fixing DESI DR2 to exact Planck 2018 ΛCDM values raises chi-squared to 31.99 across 13 observables (the 2.3-sigma DESI–Planck offset), which drops to 1.6 sigma when Planck is combined with ACT DR6 (2025)4. Second, single-bin leverage dominates out-of-sample scoring: Efstathiou (2025) showed via orthogonal geometric rotations that DESI DR2 moved closer to Planck ΛCDM than DR1 while measuring a dark-energy equation of state of −0.996 ± 0.046 at pivot redshift 0.511, and the September 2026 ELPD audit confirmed that excluding the single LRG2 bin (redshift 0.706) collapses the entire out-of-sample predictive preference for evolving dark energy to essentially zero (delta-ELPD between +0.27 and −0.04)12.
Third, supernova photometric calibration and prior sensitivity drive the highest significance claims. The DES-SN5YR catalog pairs high-redshift DES supernovae with disparate low-redshift samples below redshift 0.1 whose lowest-redshift bin disagrees by roughly 0.04 magnitudes with DESI + CMB ΛCDM predictions11. In the September 2026 audit, that single low-redshift supernova cell (where no BAO data exist) contributed −6.40 to the joint DES-SN5YR delta-ELPD; replacing DES-SN5YR with the recalibrated DES-Dovekie compilation cut that low-redshift residual to −3.46 (with Pareto diagnostic 0.75 near the sampling reliability limit) and erased the Bayesian evidence preference for evolving dark energy, whereas DESI DR2 + Planck alone yields a log Bayes factor of +0.57 ± 0.26 favoring ΛCDM (with prior-width adjustments capable of shifting log Bayes factors by roughly 3 units)12. Fourth, the July 2026 DESI DR2 Results IV Lyman-alpha full-shape analysis doubled high-redshift geometric precision at redshift 2.33 and pulled the expansion measurement back toward standard ΛCDM10. Although the tight DESI DR2 + CMB ΛCDM neutrino mass bound (<0.0642 eV; 3-sigma tension with laboratory oscillation floors) remains a genuine anomaly worth tracking10, evolving dark energy is not an established detection (Code D).
The Hubble tension likewise remains empirically undecidable (Code D) as of November 2026. Early-universe probes combining CMB acoustics and DESI DR2 cluster tightly between 67.4 and 68.5 km per second per megaparsec (Planck 2018: 67.36; DESI DR2 + CMB: 68.11 ± 0.24; P-ACT + DESI DR2: 68.43 ± 0.27)4. On the local distance ladder, SH0ES Cepheid distances corroborated by JWST crowding checks remain near 73 km per second per megaparsec (Riess et al. 2022, 2024, 2025), whereas the independent Chicago–Carnegie Hubble Program (Freedman et al. 2025) using JWST TRGB and JAGB stellar calibrations diverges at the few-percent level15. Notably, evolving dark energy in the DESI-favored quadrant shifts the inferred Hubble constant downward (to 64.5 ± 1.9 km per second per megaparsec for DESI + CMB, or 67.37 ± 0.54 with DES-Dovekie), intensifying rather than resolving the SH0ES tension10.

7.1 Exact Local Density Verification and the Unit-Conversion Trap

The canonical astrophysical and Particle Data Group benchmark for local dark-matter energy density at the Solar Circle is 0.3 GeV per cubic centimeter (modern Milky Way kinematic estimates span roughly 0.3 to 0.4 GeV per cubic centimeter)4. Applying exact CODATA 2022 conversions—one gigaelectronvolt equals 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁰ joules, one cubic centimeter equals 10⁻⁶ cubic meters, and the speed of light equals 299,792,458 meters per second—gives an exact energy density of 4.80653 × 10⁻⁵ joules per cubic meter and a mass-equivalent density of 5.348 × 10⁻²² kilograms per cubic meter (rising to 6.40871 × 10⁻⁵ joules per cubic meter and 7.131 × 10⁻²² kilograms per cubic meter at 0.4 GeV per cubic centimeter)4.
Two direct comparisons frame the severity of this number. First, confusing 0.3 GeV per cubic centimeter with a macroscopic figure such as 0.01 kilograms per cubic meter creates an overestimate factor of 1.87 × 10¹⁹—nearly nineteen orders of magnitude4. Second, expressed in proton mass equivalents, 5.35 × 10⁻²² kilograms per cubic meter equals merely 0.320 protons per cubic centimeter (319,737 proton masses per cubic meter)4. Ordinary local interstellar gas—averaging roughly one neutral or ionized hydrogen atom per cubic centimeter (1.673 × 10⁻²¹ kilograms per cubic meter)—is therefore 3.13 times denser in mass right here in the solar neighborhood than dark matter4.

7.3 Gravitational and Electromagnetic Capture Envelopes

Neither gravitational focusing nor electromagnetic scoops can bypass microscopic interaction limits. Under Bondi–Hoyle–Lyttleton gravitational capture at a typical Galactic halo relative velocity of 220 kilometers per second, the gravitational capture radius scales with twice Newton’s gravitational constant multiplied by spacecraft mass divided by relative speed squared4. For a massive 1,000-metric-ton (10⁶ kg) spacecraft, that capture radius is 2.76 × 10⁻¹⁵ meters—comparable to a single atomic nucleus—yielding a gravitational capture cross section of 2.39 × 10⁻²⁹ square meters and an accretion rate of 2.81 × 10⁻⁴⁵ kilograms per second4. Even a one-trillion-kilogram asteroid accretes merely 2.81 × 10⁻³³ kilograms per second gravitationally, and the entire Sun (1.99 × 10³⁰ kg) captures only 1.11 × 10⁴ kilograms per second4.
Electromagnetic scoop capture fails just as decisively. Canonical dark-matter candidates (WIMPs, axions, sterile neutrinos) are electrically neutral and completely transparent to magnetic scoops. Even speculative millicharged dark matter sitting at the 2025 PDG cosmological upper bound (charge fraction 3.5 × 10⁻⁷ at 1 GeV)6 exhibits a charge-to-mass ratio nearly three million times weaker than a proton’s—while the exact same magnetic scoop would capture ordinary local interstellar hydrogen gas (3.13 times denser and carrying full unit electric charge) millions of times more efficiently, overwhelming any dark-matter intake with baryonic plasma ram drag.

7.4 Momentum Budget, Intake Drag, Heating, Shielding, and Net Thrust Upper Bounds

Momentum conservation dictates that sweeping up ambient dark matter at relative speed imposes an unavoidable intake ram drag equal to mass throughput multiplied by relative inflow speed, leaving a net forward thrust equal to mass throughput multiplied by the difference between effective exhaust speed and inflow speed. Evaluating both non-annihilating kinetic reaction-mass propulsion and an idealized complete mass-to-energy annihilation engine bounds the entire physical design space4.
In the non-annihilating kinetic regime, captured dark matter is accelerated rearward by an onboard power supply; net thrust turns positive only when exhaust velocity exceeds the 220 km per second Galactic inflow velocity (otherwise the craft experiences pure drag)4. Even assuming an unphysical 100% capture efficiency across a massive one-square-kilometer collector accelerating captured mass to 1,000 kilometers per second, mass throughput reaches just 1.18 × 10⁻¹⁰ kilograms per second, yielding a net kinetic thrust of 9.18 × 10⁻⁵ newtons (roughly the weight of a single grain of sand)4. Even if that square-kilometer collector weighed only one gram per square meter (1,000 kg total foil mass, excluding the power plant and thruster), maximum acceleration would be 9.18 × 10⁻⁸ meters per second squared—and ambient local interstellar hydrogen ram drag per square meter (8.10 × 10⁻¹¹ newtons per square meter) nearly cancels the idealized kinetic dark-matter thrust per square meter (9.18 × 10⁻¹¹ newtons per square meter, a drag-to-thrust ratio of 0.88)4.
In the idealized complete mass-to-energy annihilation regime (assuming self-annihilating particles converted at 100% efficiency into perfectly collimated rearward photons traveling at the speed of light), we obtain the absolute relativistic thrust ceiling. Under the physically permitted LZ 40 GeV coherent tungsten limit (single-pass probability 5.87 × 10⁻¹⁶ across a 100-square-meter, 1-meter-thick tungsten slab weighing 1,925 metric tons), mass throughput is 6.90 × 10⁻³⁰ kilograms per second, intake drag is 1.52 × 10⁻²⁴ newtons, absolute maximum annihilation thrust is 2.07 × 10⁻²¹ newtons, and released annihilation power is 6.20 × 10⁻¹³ watts, accelerating the tungsten slab at 1.07 × 10⁻²⁷ meters per second squared (requiring roughly 3 × 10¹⁹ years—more than two billion times the age of the Universe—to change speed by one meter per second). Relaxing capture efficiency ~250-fold to 1.45 × 10⁻¹³ still yields only 5.11 × 10⁻¹⁹ newtons and 1.53 × 10⁻¹⁰ watts4.
Most devastating of all is the species-independent idealized local ceiling: repealing all particle-physics bounds to grant impossible 100% capture efficiency and 100% photon annihilation over 100 square meters at 220 km per second yields just 3.53 × 10⁻⁶ newtons (3.5 micronewtons) and 1.06 kilowatts—a surface thrust density of 3.53 × 10⁻⁸ newtons per square meter, which is 129 times weaker than absorbing solar radiation pressure at 1 AU (4.54 × 10⁻⁶ newtons per square meter) and 257 times weaker than a reflective solar sail4. Furthermore, generating even one newton of annihilation thrust requires collimating or rejecting 300 megawatts of hard gamma radiation and relativistic secondary particles, imposing severe thermal radiator and radiation-shielding mass penalties. Macroscopic thrust appears only by combining impossible unity capture efficiency with science-fiction engineering scales—such as a planetary-scale million-square-kilometer scoop (10¹² square meters, yielding 3.53 × 10⁴ newtons and 10.6 terawatts) or an extreme Galactic Center density spike of one million GeV per cubic centimeter (which still produces only 1.70 × 10⁻⁸ newtons for a square-kilometer collector under realistic LZ-bounded capture efficiency)4.

9. Discussion, Falsification Criteria, and Limitations

9.1 Synthesizing the Two-Regime Astrophysics Puzzle

This audit exposes a pronounced scale asymmetry in gravitational physics. On galaxy scales (kiloparsecs and ultra-low accelerations), the tight Radial Acceleration Relation and the superior parameter economy of one-parameter relational laws over basic two-parameter cold-dark-matter halos constitute a genuine empirical puzzle that ΛCDM addresses only through intricate baryonic feedback tuning1. Upon transitioning to cluster and cosmological scales (megaparsecs to gigaparsecs), however, the evidential balance reverses decisively: pure visible-baryon relational laws fail on merging clusters without hidden collisionless mass2, lack a cosmological completion35, and provide no mechanism to eliminate dark energy—while every zero-dark-energy distance–redshift imitation evaluated on DESI DR2 BAO collapses by hundreds to thousands of chi-squared units4.
Covariant effective field theories such as AeST (H2) establish that a relativistic gravitational action can unite MONDian galactic dynamics with CMB acoustic peaks and exact light-speed gravitational waves7. Yet AeST achieves this unification by introducing dynamical vector and scalar fields that effectively act as a cosmological dark-matter fluid while retaining a cosmological constant and remaining untested in full non-linear cluster mergers. Under the ArcSecs mandate to distinguish mathematical representations from established physical proof, neither ΛCDM nor AeST yet provides an unblemished, tension-free synthesis across every scale.

9.2 Explicit Falsification Criteria

To keep every competing hypothesis empirically falsifiable, we establish four binding experimental thresholds:

  1. Falsification of H1 (Pure Relational Dynamics): Cross-team consensus confirming the absence of a ~20% low-acceleration orbital boost in Gaia DR3/DR4 wide binaries after realistic triple-star modeling, or demonstration that wide-field weak-lensing shear in the Bullet Cluster and relaxed clusters exceeds the maximum IGIMF dark-remnant baryon budget compatible with Big Bang Nucleosynthesis.
  2. Falsification of H2 (Covariant AeST): Failure of full 3D non-linear cosmological and merging-cluster simulations of AeST to match observed cluster abundances and lensing–gas offsets without dynamical instabilities, or confirmed direct laboratory detection of a reproducible non-gravitational dark-matter recoil or axion signal.
  3. Falsification of Flat ΛCDM (H0): Convergence of future DESI full-survey BAO plus independently recalibrated supernova catalogs (DES-Dovekie, Pantheon+, Rubin/LSST, Euclid) to a greater than 5-sigma out-of-sample predictive preference (jointly in Bayesian evidence and ELPD) for dark-energy evolution that survives removal of every individual redshift bin, or persistence of a greater than 5-sigma Hubble tension after Cepheid, TRGB, and JAGB anchors converge.
  4. Falsification of Propulsion Impossibility (H4): Would require both experimental discovery of a dark-sector species violating all current direct-detection and cosmological decoupling limits by more than 15 orders of magnitude and a local dark-matter overdensity exceeding the Solar Circle benchmark by more than 10 orders of magnitude—conditions excluded by Galactic kinematics and planetary ephemerides.

9.3 Study Limitations

Three explicit limitations qualify this investigation. First, raw per-galaxy SPARC rotation-curve tables were not locally executed for a fresh 70/30 stratified split; all SPARC comparisons derive from verified peer-reviewed and 2026 benchmark publications1. Second, full joint Planck/ACT Boltzmann likelihood chains and supernova covariance matrices (Pantheon+, Union3, DES-Dovekie) were not re-run locally; our executed cosmological fits cover the exact 13 × 13 DESI DR2 BAO covariance matrix4, augmented by audited 2025–2026 joint analyses10. Third, non-perturbative inhomogeneous backreaction cosmologies (Timescape) were not integrated in our FLRW likelihood pipeline and remain to be tested against full CMB plus BAO covariance matrices50.

10. Conclusions: Separate Verdicts on Astrophysics and Propulsion

Verdict 1: Astrophysical Explanatory Power

Mixed, Scale-Dependent Verdict — Neither Pure Relational Dynamics Nor Unmodified Halo Models Close Every Empirical Gap. On galaxy scales, relational acceleration laws (H1 and the quasi-static limit of H2 — Code B; wide-binary test — Code D) demonstrate remarkable predictive economy across the 175-galaxy SPARC catalog, outperforming basic two-parameter NFW halos in average fit quality and avoiding NFW’s 19% boundary-limited failure rate1. Nevertheless, feedback-modified ΛCDM halos narrow that gap, MOND exhibits mass-to-light tension on dwarfs such as IC 2574, and Gaia DR3 wide-binary tests remain actively contested3. On cluster scales, pure visible-baryon relational gravity without unseen collisionless mass is excluded (Code C) by the eight-sigma lensing–gas offset in the Bullet Cluster and peers; 2026 JWST re-analyses indicate that MOND augmented by IGIMF compact dark stellar remnants can match core strong-lensing budgets, leaving remnant-augmented H1/H2 undecidable (Code D) pending cluster-wide weak-lensing and nucleosynthesis tests2. On cosmological scales, pure H1 lacks a cosmological completion; covariant AeST (H2) reproduces CMB acoustics without particle dark matter while retaining a cosmological constant7; and our executed DESI DR2 BAO covariance fits decisively exclude (Code C) all simple zero-dark-energy distance–redshift imitations (flat coasting, tired light, open CDM, Einstein–de Sitter) by 183 to 15,214 chi-squared units4. Simultaneously, seven-fold out-of-sample cross-validation (flat ΛCDM 13.22 versus evolving dark energy 13.76), Monte Carlo null calibration, the July 2026 DESI Lyman-alpha full-shape shift back toward ΛCDM, and independent 2025–2026 ELPD/Bayesian audits demonstrate that evolving dark energy is not an established detection (Code D)4.

Verdict 2: Engineered Substrate-Capture Propulsion Feasibility

Unambiguous Physical Impossibility Within All Experimentally Permitted Regimes (Code C). Dimensional verification establishes that the canonical local dark-matter energy density (0.3 GeV per cubic centimeter) equals 5.35 × 10⁻²² kilograms per cubic meter—nearly nineteen orders of magnitude smaller than 0.01 kilograms per cubic meter and 3.13 times sparser than ordinary local interstellar hydrogen gas4. Across every species-specific experimental bound (LZ 2025 spin-independent WIMP limit of 2.2 × 10⁻⁴⁸ square centimeters at 40 GeV, sub-GeV electron bounds, CMB millicharged bounds, cluster self-interaction bounds, and axion haloscope constraints), single-pass capture probabilities in a dense one-meter solid tungsten slab lie between 10⁻²³ and 10⁻¹⁰ (at most ~4 × 10⁻¹⁵ for coherent WIMP–tungsten scattering, corresponding to a mean free path of 0.03 to 0.18 light-years of solid tungsten), while Bondi–Hoyle–Lyttleton gravitational capture by a 1,000-tonne craft yields 2.81 × 10⁻⁴⁵ kilograms per second4. Physically permitted thrust for a 100-square-meter collector sits at or below 2.07 × 10⁻²¹ newtons—more than twenty orders of magnitude below usable spacecraft propulsion. Even repealing particle physics to allow unphysical 100% capture efficiency and 100% photon annihilation caps local thrust density at 3.53 × 10⁻⁸ newtons per square meter, which is 129 times weaker than ordinary solar radiation pressure at 1 AU, while idealized kinetic dark-matter propulsion is nearly canceled by ambient interstellar gas drag4. No physically viable dark-matter propulsion design envelope exists.

Appendix D: Plain-English ArcSecs Advocacy Summary

Why Question the Dark Sector—And What the Evidence Actually Shows
For nearly half a century, mainstream astrophysics has explained two grand mysteries by invoking invisible ingredients: dark matter to hold spinning galaxies and galaxy clusters together, and dark energy to push the expanding universe faster and faster. The ArcSecs perspective begins from disciplined scientific skepticism: before concluding that roughly 95% of the cosmos consists of unseen particles and mysterious vacuum energy that no laboratory has yet isolated, we should test whether we have misunderstood how gravity, matter, and light relate to one another—and whether any real dark medium around us could ever fuel future spacecraft.
Our independent audit through November 2026 tested both questions against the sharpest astronomical and particle-physics measurements available. The result is neither a sweeping victory for dark-sector skepticism nor a rubber stamp for conventional assumptions. Instead, nature delivers a clear split verdict.
Where Relational Mechanisms Shine: Spinning Galaxies. Inside spiral and irregular galaxies, visible stars and gas dictate the total gravitational pull with uncanny precision. Across the 175-galaxy SPARC survey, simple relational laws governed by one universal acceleration threshold routinely match galaxy rotation speeds better—and with fewer adjustable knobs—than standard dark-matter halo models that require multiple free settings per galaxy1. That tight coupling between visible starlight and orbital motion remains the strongest clue that galaxy-scale gravity or galaxy formation is not yet completely understood.
Where Simple Alternatives Hit a Wall: Colliding Clusters and the Expanding Universe. Stepping beyond individual galaxies, pure visible-matter modified gravity runs into formidable reality checks. When two giant galaxy clusters collide in the Bullet Cluster, the hot gas—most of the ordinary visible matter—slams to a halt near the middle, while gravitational lensing shows that most of the mass keeps flying outward with the galaxies2. Even under modified gravity, the latest James Webb Space Telescope observations show you still need hidden, non-colliding mass traveling alongside those galaxies—whether conventional dark matter or vast swarms of dark stellar remnants like black holes and neutron stars2.
On the grandest cosmic scales, the DESI three-dimensional map of more than 14 million galaxies and quasars renders an even sharper judgment10. Simple attempts to explain away cosmic acceleration as an optical or geometric illusion—such as “tired light” or a steadily coasting universe—fail our statistical tests catastrophically4. Meanwhile, recent headlines claiming that dark energy is weakening over time turn out to be premature: when we test the DESI data by withholding one cosmic distance slice at a time, the simple unchanging cosmological constant actually predicts unseen data slightly better than evolving dark energy, and DESI’s newest July 2026 measurements from distant hydrogen clouds shifted right back toward the standard model4.
The Verdict on Dark-Matter Spacecraft Propulsion: Impossible. Finally, could a starship harvest a real dark-matter cloud in our solar neighborhood as rocket fuel? The physics closes that door completely. Even if dark matter pervades the room around you, its mass density is unimaginably thin—about three times sparser than ordinary interstellar hydrogen gas, and nearly twenty billion billion times less dense than a careless unit-conversion error would suggest4. Worse still, underground detectors like LUX-ZEPLIN show that dark matter passes through solid metal like a ghost: a particle would have to travel through nearly two-tenths of a light-year of solid tungsten just to strike a single nucleus4. Even if magic technology captured 100% of every dark-matter particle crossing a giant scoop and annihilated it into pure rearward light, the resulting push would still be 129 times weaker than ordinary sunlight pressing on a solar sail4.
The Bottom Line: The ArcSecs instinct to scrutinize unseen substances pays real scientific dividends—spotlighting genuine puzzles in galaxy dynamics and guarding against premature claims of evolving dark energy. Equally, honest science means accepting when a bold proposal fails. Galaxy-scale relational laws merit continued testing, cosmic acceleration is real rather than a simple distance illusion, and dark matter will never power a spacecraft.
